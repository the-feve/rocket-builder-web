Day 3

Stability: the balance point and the push point

Time: about 90 minutes. Worksheet: W3 Swing test.

Objectives

  • Find a rocket's balance point (centre of gravity, CG) by balancing it.
  • Estimate its push point (centre of pressure, CP) with a cardboard cut-out.
  • Explain why the balance point must be in front of the push point for a straight flight.
  • Measure the gap in calibers and use the stability score: 1–2 cal is just right.
  • Test stability with the string swing test, and fix an unstable rocket.

Vocabulary

  • Stable: flies straight, nose first. If it tilts, it swings back.
  • Unstable: wobbles, tumbles or loops.
  • Balance point (CG, centre of gravity): where the rocket balances on your finger. Rocket Builder shows it as a blue dot.
  • Push point (CP, centre of pressure): where the air's push acts, on average. Rocket Builder shows it as a red dot.
  • Caliber (cal): one body width, used as a ruler. A rocket 2 cm wide has 1 cal = 2 cm.
  • Stability score (stability margin): how far the push point is behind the balance point, in calibers.
  • Weathercocking: a very stable rocket turning into the wind, like a weathervane.

Materials

Household:

  • A paper-towel tube (or roll a tube of cardstock about 4–5 cm wide and 25–30 cm long)
  • Cardstock or a cereal box for a nose cone and fins
  • Modeling clay (about a walnut-sized lump)
  • String, about 1.5 m; tape, scissors, ruler, pencil
  • Scrap cardboard for the cut-out; a ruler or the edge of a table to balance on
  • Your straw rocket from Day 2

To buy: nothing.

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

Have you ever thrown a dart or shot an arrow? The feathers at the back keep it flying point-first. Rocket fins do the same job. Fins are a rocket's feathers.

Every rocket has two special points.

The balance point is where the rocket balances on your finger. Scientists call it the centre of gravity, or CG. In Rocket Builder it's the blue dot. Weight in the nose moves it forward. The motor is heavy and sits at the back, so it pulls the balance point backward.

The push point is where the air pushes on the rocket, on average. Scientists call it the centre of pressure, or CP. In Rocket Builder it's the red dot. Fins are big and flat and sit at the back, so they pull the push point backward.

Here's the rule: the blue dot must be in front of the red dot.

Why? Imagine the rocket tilts a little. Air hits the side. Because the push point is behind the balance point, the air pushes the tail more than the nose. That swings the tail back in line, and the nose points straight again. It's just like a weathervane, or the feathers on an arrow.

If the push point is in front of the balance point, the air swings the nose the wrong way. The rocket tumbles, or loops back toward the ground. That's dangerous.

We measure the gap in calibers. One caliber is one body width. Rocket Builder's stability score works like this:

  • Under 1 cal: wobbly. It might tumble. The app won't let you download it.
  • 1 to 2 cal: just right!
  • 2 to 3 cal: very stable. Still fine.
  • Over 3 cal: too stable. It turns into the wind like a weathervane and flies off at an angle.

The app checks the score with the heaviest motor of your size loaded, because the motor is heavy and sits at the back. That's the worst case.

How do you fix a wobbly rocket? Make the fins bigger (moves the push point back), make the rocket taller, or add a little weight in the nose (moves the balance point forward).

Activity 1: Find the two points

The balance point (CG)

  1. Build a test rocket: a paper-towel tube body, a cardstock cone nose taped on top, and 3 or 4 cardstock fins (about 6 cm long and 5 cm tall) taped at the bottom.
  2. Lay it across one finger (or the edge of a ruler). Slide it until it balances.
  3. Mark that spot with a blue pencil dot or a sticker. Measure from the nose tip to the dot. Write it down.

The push point (CP), the cardboard cut-out way

  1. Lay the rocket on scrap cardboard and trace its outline from the side (nose, body and one fin on each side, as it looks from the side).
  2. Cut out the shape.
  3. Balance the cut-out on a ruler edge. The balance point of the flat shape is a rough estimate of the push point. (It's a cautious estimate: the real push point is usually a little further back.)
  4. Mark it on the real rocket with a red dot. Measure from the nose tip.

The score

Score (cal) = (red-dot distance − blue-dot distance) ÷ body width.

Example: blue dot 18 cm from the tip, red dot 27 cm, body 4.5 cm wide. (27 − 18) ÷ 4.5 = 2 cal.

Activity 2 ★ The string swing test

Real rocketeers use this test before a first flight. You'll test four versions of your rocket.

  1. Go outside, or to a big room with nothing breakable. Nobody stands within 3 m.
  2. Find the balance point again (it changes when you change the rocket!).
  3. Tie the string around the rocket at the balance point and tape it so it can't slide. The rocket should hang level.
  4. Hold the string about 1 m from the rocket. Swing the rocket in a circle around your head (or beside you, if that's easier).
  5. Watch: does the nose lead the way? Write stable, wobbly or tumbles.
  6. Sometimes a rocket needs a gentle start: hold it nose-first along the circle, then let go as you swing.

Test these four versions. Find the new balance point each time.

Version Change What to expect
A No fins, no clay Often tumbles or flies sideways
B Fins, no clay Might be stable or wobbly
C Fins plus clay inside the nose Stable: nose leads
D Fins plus clay taped at the tail (like a heavy motor) Wobbly or tumbles

Worksheet

In the worksheets PDF (or as a web page). Print at 100%.

W3 Swing test: label the balance point and the push point on a drawing, the table for versions A–D with distances and scores, and two questions.

Discussion questions

  1. Why does clay in the nose help, but clay at the tail hurt?
  2. Why does Rocket Builder check the score with the heaviest motor loaded?
  3. An arrow, a dart and a badminton shuttlecock all fly point-first. Where is the weight, and where are the "fins"?
  4. Why is "more stable" not always better? (Think about wind.)
  5. If your rocket is wobbly, which fix would you try first, and why?

Quick check

  1. Which dot must be in front: blue (balance point) or red (push point)?
  2. Name two ways to move the push point backward.
  3. A rocket's body is 2 cm wide. Its push point is 3 cm behind its balance point. What's its score?
  4. Is a score of 0.5 cal OK to fly?
  5. What happens to a rocket with a score of 4 cal on a windy day?
Answer key
  1. Blue (the balance point) must be in front of red (the push point).
  2. Bigger fins; fins further back (swept back); a longer body. (Any two.)
  3. 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 cal: just right.
  4. No. Under 1 cal is wobbly and could tumble. Rocket Builder won't let you download it.
  5. It turns into the wind (weathercocks) and flies off at an angle.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): do versions A and C only. Skip the cut-out and the score math; just compare "nose leads" and "tumbles". Grades 5–6: add the cut-out and work out the score with a calculator.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • Do all four versions and work out the score for each.
  • A Rocket Builder rocket: balance point 253 mm from the tip, push point 289 mm, body 21.4 mm wide. Score = (289 − 253) ÷ 21.4 = 1.7 cal (rounded).
  • Why does the cut-out estimate the push point at all? The air pushes on the side area, so the middle of the side area is a fair guess. It's what the push point would be if the rocket flew sideways, which is why it's cautious.
  • Find out about the Barrowman method, the hand calculation Rocket Builder uses (developed by James Barrowman in the 1960s).

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): open Rocket Builder, build a rocket and find the blue and red dots. Read the score. Try a different fin shape and build again. Did the score change? Then press ▶ Watch it launch: a stable rocket flies nose-first.

Printed-rocket track (12+): the same on screen. On Day 9 you'll swing-test your real printed rocket, with a motor put in by a grown-up (no igniter). Same steps.
