Day 9

Safety, launch prep and graphing your data

Time: about 60 minutes (younger kids) to 90 minutes (12+). Worksheet: W9 Safety and preflight, plus W9b Graph your week.

Objectives

  • Follow the paper-rocket safety rules: glasses on, never aim at anyone, the adult runs fizzy rockets.
  • Explain the main rules of the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code and why each one matters, and that motor rockets are 12+, with grown-ups handling motors, igniters and the launch key.
  • Know each job on the launch team, and practise a countdown (12+: and a misfire drill).
  • Make a clear graph from this week's data, with a title, labels and units, and describe what it shows.
  • 12+ track: run preflight checks on the real rocket.

Vocabulary

  • Safety code: a set of rules everyone follows to keep a launch safe.
  • Launch controller: the box with a button that sends electricity to the igniter.
  • Safety key: a removable key in the controller. No key, no launch.
  • Misfire: when the button is pressed but the motor doesn't light.
  • Launch Control Officer (LCO): the grown-up in charge of the launch button and the countdown.
  • Range Safety Officer (RSO): the grown-up who checks every rocket before it goes on the pad.
  • Preflight check: a checklist done before every flight.
  • Mean: the average. Range: biggest minus smallest.

Materials

  • W9 and W9b worksheets, graph paper or the worksheet grid, ruler, coloured pencils
  • Your data sheets from Days 1–8
  • Under 12: a stomp rocket and safety glasses for the countdown practice
  • 12+ track: the assembled rocket, a kitchen scale, string, one motor (a grown-up handles it), the launch controller with no igniter attached, the weather forecast

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

Every rocket launch has rules, even paper ones. Here are ours:

  • Glasses on for straw, stomp and fizzy rockets.
  • Never aim a rocket at a person, a pet or a window.
  • Wait for "launch!" Nobody stomps or blows until the countdown ends and everyone is behind the launcher.
  • Fizzy rockets are run by a grown-up. The grown-up keeps the tablets.
  • Never chase a rocket into a road, a tree or a power line.

Real model rockets with motors are for ages 12 and up, and they follow a bigger set of rules: the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code. That's why model rocketry is one of the safest hobbies there is. Let's go through the big ones and why they matter.

  1. Grown-ups handle motors and igniters. Motors contain fuel that burns very hot. Only grown-ups load them, put in igniters and hold the launch key.
  2. Use only real model rocket motors, from the store, exactly as they come. Never open, change or reload one.
  3. Launch with an electric controller and a safety key. The key stays in a grown-up's pocket until the rocket is ready and everyone is back.
  4. Count down out loud from 5. Everyone looks at the rocket. Everyone stands at least 15 feet (about 5 m) back.
  5. If it doesn't launch (a misfire), wait 60 seconds. Take out the key first. A motor can light late.
  6. Use a launch rod pointing nearly straight up, with a blast deflector, and clear the dry grass. The flame is hot.
  7. Launch in a big open field, with no trees, power lines or buildings nearby, and not in winds over 20 mph.
  8. Never launch at anything: not at targets, into clouds, near airplanes, or over people.
  9. Use a parachute or streamer, with flame-resistant wadding.
  10. Never chase a rocket into danger. Not into a power line, a tall tree or a road. And never try to catch one.

On launch day, everyone has a job. The grown-ups are the Launch Control Officer (the countdown and the button) and the Range Safety Officer (checks each rocket). Kids can be the countdown caller, the trackers with inclinometers, the recorder who fills in the flight log, and the recovery crew who go to get the rocket once the LCO says it's safe.

Activity 1 ★ Safety drills

  1. Is it safe? On W9, read each launch-day story and decide: safe or not safe? Which rule does it follow or break?
  2. Countdown practice (under 12): set up the stomp rocket. Everyone puts on glasses and stands behind the launcher. The countdown caller says "Range is clear! 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" and the stomper waits for "launch!" Take turns at each job.
  3. Countdown and misfire drill (12+): with no motor and no igniter, set up the pad and controller. The grown-up holds the key. Practise: "Range is clear! Rocket is armed. 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" Then the grown-up pretends the rocket didn't go, removes the key, and everyone counts 60 seconds out loud, staying back, before anyone walks up to the pad.
  4. Wind check: look at tomorrow's forecast. Write the wind speed. Is it under 20 mph? (Lighter is better: under 10 mph means less drift.)

Activity 2: Graph your week

  1. Pick one of your data sets: balloon rocket (Day 1), launch angles (Day 2), nose cones (Day 4), the design log (Day 6) or the parachute drop (Day 7).
  2. Decide on the graph type: a bar graph for categories (nose shape), a line graph for numbers that change step by step (height, canopy size).
  3. Make the graph on W9b with: a title, labels on both axes, units (cm, s, m, cal), evenly spaced numbers, and neat bars or dots.
  4. Find the mean and the range for one group of trials.
  5. Write two sentences: "As ___ went up, ___ went ___." and "One thing that might have made my data less accurate is ___."

Activity 3 (12+ track): Preflight checks on your real rocket

A grown-up handles the motor in steps 3–5. No igniter today.

  1. Weigh the rocket without a motor. Compare with the app's mass in the report.
  2. Look it over: fins firm, nose slides off smoothly, shock cord tied tightly, parachute not torn.
  3. Balance point with motor: a grown-up puts a motor in the motor tube (no igniter). Balance the rocket on one finger. Measure from the nose tip to that spot. Is it close to the app's CG? Is it in front of the CP?
  4. Swing test: swing the rocket (motor in, parachute packed, no igniter) on a string tied at the balance point, as on Day 3. It must fly nose-first.
  5. A grown-up takes the motor out and puts it back in its pack.
  6. Field check: open the app's How big a launch field? card. Compare it with your field. If your field is too small, choose a smaller motor that still gets a ✓, or go to a club launch.

Discussion questions

  1. Which safety rule do you think is most important? Why?
  2. Why do we wait a full 60 seconds after a misfire?
  3. Why do we count down out loud?
  4. Why should you never try to get a rocket out of a power line?
  5. Which of your graphs shows the clearest trend? Why do you think so?

Quick check

  1. How far back must everyone stand for a D motor or smaller?
  2. After a misfire, what are the two things the grown-up does before anyone goes near the pad?
  3. What's the highest wind speed you can launch in?
  4. Who handles motors and igniters?
  5. Your three trials were 4 m, 5 m and 6 m. What's the mean? The range?
Answer key
  1. At least 15 feet (about 5 m).
  2. Takes out the safety key (or disconnects the battery), then waits 60 seconds.
  3. 20 mph (and less wind is better).
  4. A grown-up.
  5. Mean = (4 + 5 + 6) ÷ 3 = 5 m. Range = 6 − 4 = 2 m.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): do the "Is it safe?" stories aloud and talk about the paper-rocket rules first. Graph one data set as a bar graph.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • Make a scatter plot from the design log (height vs score, or span vs score). Draw a line of best fit by eye. Is the relationship linear? Where does it bend?
  • Use the drop-test data: plot canopy area (side × side) against descent rate. Does doubling the area halve the speed? (It shouldn't! Speed goes with 1 ÷ √area, so doubling the area slows it by only about 30%.)
  • Field math: a rocket's predicted altitude is 250 m and it comes down at 4 m/s in a 4.5 m/s breeze. How far does it drift? (250 ÷ 4 × 4.5 ≈ 280 m.) Is a 120 m field big enough? (No.)

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): go through the paper-launch checklist on W9. Pack stomp rockets, fizzy rocket supplies (the adult keeps the tablets), safety glasses, inclinometers and the paper-rocket launch log.

Printed-rocket track (12+): pack your launch box tonight (see the list on Day 10). A grown-up makes sure the motors, igniters, wadding and the safety key are in it.
