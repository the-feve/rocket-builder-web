Day 7

Parachutes and descent rate

Time: about 90 minutes. Worksheet: W7 Parachute drop test.

Objectives

  • Explain how a parachute works, using drag and weight.
  • Explain terminal speed: a falling object stops speeding up when drag equals weight.
  • Run a drop test, calculate descent rate (metres per second) and graph canopy size against fall time.
  • Explain why a rocket's parachute must be big enough, but not too big.

Vocabulary

  • Recovery: bringing the rocket back down safely so it can fly again.
  • Parachute: a canopy that makes lots of drag to slow a fall.
  • Canopy: the cloth or plastic part of the parachute.
  • Shroud lines: the strings from the canopy to the rocket.
  • Descent rate: how fast something falls, in metres per second (m/s). Descent rate = height ÷ time.
  • Terminal speed: the steady speed a falling object reaches when drag balances weight.
  • Drift: how far the wind carries the rocket sideways while it comes down.
  • Streamer: a long strip of plastic or paper that flutters and slows a very light rocket.

Materials

Household:

  • Plastic grocery bags or a trash bag
  • String, thread or dental floss
  • Tape, scissors, ruler, a marker
  • A weight for each parachute: 2 or 3 metal washers, or binder clips (they must be the same for every parachute)
  • A stopwatch (or a phone)
  • A safe drop spot 2–3 m high: an upstairs landing, a deck, or a grown-up standing on a sturdy step stool holding it high. A grown-up does any dropping from a height.
  • Tape measure
  • Coffee filters (optional quick demo)

To buy: nothing.

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

At the top of its flight, the rocket's ejection charge pops off the nose and pushes out the parachute. Now the rocket has to come down gently, so it doesn't break and can fly again.

When something falls, gravity pulls it down and it speeds up. But the faster it falls, the more drag it feels. Soon the drag is as big as the weight. Now the forces are balanced, so it stops speeding up and falls at a steady speed. That's called terminal speed.

A parachute makes a lot of drag, so the forces balance at a slow speed. A bigger canopy catches more air, so it falls slower. A heavier load needs more drag to balance it, so it falls faster.

Rocket Builder picks a parachute that brings your rocket down at about 4.5 m/s or less. That's like jogging speed. Between 3 and 5 m/s lands without breaking anything.

Why not use a huge parachute and come down super slowly? Because of the wind. The longer the rocket hangs in the air, the further the wind carries it. It might land in a tree, on a road or outside the field. Hobby parachutes come in sizes of 12, 15, 18, 24, 30 and 36 inches across. The app picks the smallest one that's slow enough. Very light rockets (under about 40 g after the motor burns) can use a streamer instead.

Quick demo (optional): drop one coffee filter and a stack of four from the same height. The stack has the same shape but four times the weight, so it falls faster. Each one still reaches a steady speed.

Activity 1 ★ Parachute drop test

Make three parachutes

  1. Cut three squares from a plastic bag: 20 cm, 30 cm and 40 cm on each side.
  2. Cut 4 strings, each 30 cm long, for each parachute. (Use the same length for all of them.)
  3. Tape one string to each corner. Put a small square of tape under each corner first so it doesn't tear.
  4. Tie the 4 loose ends together to the weight (washers or a binder clip). Each parachute gets the same weight.

Drop and time

  1. Measure the drop height (from the release point to the floor). Write it down.
  2. Hold the parachute by the middle of the canopy, open, with the weight hanging down.
  3. Say "3, 2, 1, drop!" Start the stopwatch on "drop" and stop it when the weight touches the floor.
  4. Do 3 trials for each parachute. Also time the weight alone, with no parachute.
  5. Average the times. Then find each descent rate: height ÷ average time.
  6. Make a graph: canopy size along the bottom, average fall time up the side.

Part B (optional): change the weight. Use the 30 cm parachute with 1, 2 and then 4 washers.

What to expect

the bigger the canopy, the longer the fall. The weight with no parachute lands in well under a second. More washers fall faster. From a short height the times are close together, so careful timing matters; a higher drop gives better data.

Activity 2: Your rocket's parachute

  1. In Rocket Builder, build your design and find the Which parachute? card. Write down the size (inches) and the descent speed (m/s).
  2. Work out how long it will take to come down from the predicted height: time = height ÷ descent speed.
  3. Press ▶ Watch it launch and watch the parachute drift.
  4. Drift: in a 10 mph breeze (about 4.5 m/s), how far will it drift? Drift = time × 4.5.

Example: a rocket predicted to reach 140 m, with a 15-inch parachute falling at 3.8 m/s. Time = 140 ÷ 3.8 ≈ 37 s. Drift = 37 × 4.5 ≈ 166 m. That's further than the 60 m minimum field for its B motor! This is why the app suggests a field bigger than the minimum.

12+ track: get or make the parachute size the app says. Practise folding it: fold it in half, then in half again, wrap the lines loosely around it, and roll it so it slides easily into the body tube. Flame-resistant wadding goes in below it on launch day. A grown-up checks the packing.

Discussion questions

  1. Why did the bigger parachutes fall slower?
  2. What happened to the speed when you added weight? Why?
  3. Why might a parachute that's too big be a problem?
  4. How could you test parachutes more fairly, or more accurately?
  5. What could make a real parachute fail to open? (Packed too tight, tangled lines, melted by hot gas without wadding, ejection too early or late.)

Quick check

  1. A parachute falls 3 m in 1.5 seconds. What's its descent rate?
  2. Bigger canopy, same weight: faster or slower fall?
  3. When a falling parachute reaches a steady speed, which two forces are balanced?
  4. Rocket Builder aims for about what descent speed?
  5. A rocket comes down from 100 m at 4 m/s in a 4.5 m/s wind. How far does it drift?
Answer key
  1. 3 ÷ 1.5 = 2 m/s.
  2. Slower.
  3. Drag (up) and weight (down).
  4. About 4.5 m/s or less (3–5 m/s lands safely).
  5. Time = 100 ÷ 4 = 25 s. Drift = 25 × 4.5 ≈ 113 m (about 110 m).

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): use two parachute sizes and compare times only (skip descent rate). For the app part, just write down the parachute size, then press ▶ Watch it launch and see how far the wind carries the rocket.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • The descent-rate formula: speed = √( 2 × mass × g ÷ (air density × Cd × area) ). With g = 9.8 m/s², air density 1.2 kg/m³ and Cd about 0.75 for a flat hobby chute.
  • Check Rocket Builder: a 76 g rocket under a 15-inch (0.381 m) chute. Area = π × 0.19² ≈ 0.114 m². Speed = √(2 × 0.076 × 9.8 ÷ (1.2 × 0.75 × 0.114)) ≈ 3.8 m/s, the same as the app.
  • What happens if you double the parachute's width? The area goes up 4 times, so the speed is cut in half. If you make the rocket 4 times heavier, the speed doubles. Test it with your drop data: does a 40 cm chute fall about twice as slowly as a 20 cm one?

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): save your best drop-test parachute. On Day 10, tape it to a paper rocket and toss it high, or fold it into the nose of a stomp rocket (it opens… sometimes!).

Printed-rocket track (12+): make sure your parachute, shock cord (braided flat elastic) and wadding have arrived, or make a plastic parachute the size the app says, with strong thread lines and reinforced corners. Practise folding and packing it (Activity 2).
