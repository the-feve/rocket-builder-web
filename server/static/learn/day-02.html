Day 2

The four forces on a rocket

Time: about 75 minutes. Worksheet: W2 Forces and launch angles.

Objectives

  • Name the four forces on a rocket: thrust, weight, drag and lift, and show their directions with arrows.
  • Tell balanced forces from unbalanced forces, and say what each does to motion.
  • Describe the parts of a flight: on the pad, boost, coast, top (apogee) and coming down.
  • Build a straw rocket and test how launch angle changes how far it goes.

Vocabulary

  • Thrust: the motor's push, toward the nose.
  • Weight: gravity's pull, always straight down. Weight depends on mass: 100 g weighs about 1 N.
  • Drag: air pushing back against anything moving through it. It always points opposite to the motion.
  • Lift: a sideways push from air flowing over a fin or wing when it's tilted. On a rocket, the fins make lift that steers the nose back into line.
  • Balanced forces: forces that cancel out. The motion doesn't change.
  • Unbalanced forces: forces that don't cancel. The motion changes (speeds up, slows down or turns).
  • Net force: what's left over when you add up all the forces.
  • Apogee: the highest point of the flight.

Materials

Household:

  • Printer paper and some cardstock (or a cereal box)
  • Pencils (a little fatter than your straws), drinking straws
  • Tape, scissors, ruler
  • Tape measure, a box or a book to rest the protractor on

To buy: a protractor ($1–3), safety glasses ($3–6 each).

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

Yesterday we learned that a rocket pushes gas out the back to move forward. Today we'll look at all the forces on a rocket in flight. There are four.

Thrust is the motor's push. It points toward the nose.

Weight is gravity pulling down. It always points straight down, toward the middle of the Earth.

Drag is air pushing back. Stick your hand out of a car window and you feel it. The faster you go, the bigger the drag. It always points the opposite way to the motion.

Lift is the push that holds up a plane's wings. Rocket fins are small wings. When a rocket tilts, air hits the fins at an angle and pushes them sideways. That push swings the nose back to pointing straight. We'll see this again on Day 3.

When forces are balanced, nothing changes. A rocket on the pad has weight pulling down and the pad pushing up. They cancel, so it stays still.

When forces are unbalanced, the motion changes. When the motor fires, thrust is much bigger than weight plus drag, so the rocket speeds up. Model rockets need thrust at least 5 times their weight, so they leave the pad fast and fly straight.

Then the motor burns out. Now there's no thrust. Weight and drag both pull the rocket back, so it slows down while it coasts upward. At the very top, called apogee, it stops going up for a moment. Then the parachute comes out. The parachute makes lots of drag, so the rocket floats down slowly.

Activity 1 ★ Build paper straw rockets

  1. Cut a strip of paper about 8 cm wide and 20 cm long.
  2. Roll it tightly around a pencil and tape the seam. Slide it off. This is the body tube. It should slide easily over a straw, not too tight, not too loose.
  3. Close the top: twist or fold the end over and tape it shut. This is the nose. It must be airtight, or the air leaks out the top.
  4. Cut 3 or 4 small fins from cardstock (about 3 cm long). Tape them evenly around the bottom.
  5. Put on safety glasses. Slide the rocket onto a straw and blow one sharp puff.
Safety

everyone nearby wears glasses. Never aim at a person, pet or window.

Activity 2: Launch-angle test

Which angle sends a straw rocket furthest?

  1. Tape a protractor to the edge of a box or a book, flat side up.
  2. Hold the straw along the protractor at 15°. Blow as hard as you can, the same way each time.
  3. Measure from the launch spot to where the rocket first lands. Do 3 trials.
  4. Repeat at 30°, 45°, 60° and 75°.
  5. Find each average. Make a bar graph: angle on the bottom, distance up the side.
What to expect

somewhere near 45° usually goes furthest. With drag, the best angle is often a little lower, about 40°. Steep angles go high but not far. Low angles hit the ground too soon.

Discussion questions

  1. In which part of the flight are the forces balanced? (Two answers.)
  2. Why does the rocket keep going up after the motor stops?
  3. Why do you think rockets must push at least 5 times their weight?
  4. The launch-angle test isn't perfectly fair. Why? How could you make the "blow" the same every time? (Ideas: squeeze a plastic bottle with a straw taped in the cap, drop a book on the bottle from the same height.)
  5. Real model rockets launch almost straight up. Why don't we aim them like the straw rockets? (So they land near us, inside the field, and away from people.)

Quick check

  1. Which force always points straight down?
  2. Which force always points opposite to the motion?
  3. During the coast, which forces act on the rocket?
  4. A rocket's thrust is 6 N and its weight is 1 N. Is it at least 5 times its weight?
  5. Under a parachute the rocket falls at a steady speed. Are the forces balanced or unbalanced?
Answer key
  1. Weight (gravity).
  2. Drag.
  3. Weight and drag (and a little lift from the fins if it tilts). No thrust.
  4. Yes: 6 ÷ 1 = 6, and 6 is more than 5.
  5. Balanced: drag up equals weight down, so the speed doesn't change.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): draw the arrows for "on the pad" and "boost" only. Use 3 angles (30°, 45°, 60°). Grades 3–4: measure in whole centimetres and compare the longest trials instead of averaging.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • Find the net force: a rocket weighs 1 N, the motor pushes 6 N and drag is 0.5 N. Net force = 6 − 1 − 0.5 = 4.5 N up. Its mass is 0.1 kg, so a = F ÷ m = 4.5 ÷ 0.1 = 45 m/s², about 4½ times the pull of gravity.
  • Draw arrows to scale (1 cm = 1 N) for boost and coast.
  • Why does 45° work best with no air? (The rocket stays up longest at high angles but goes sideways fastest at low angles; 45° balances the two.)

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): in Rocket Builder, build any rocket and press ▶ Watch it launch. Call out each part as it happens: liftoff, boost, coast, top (apogee), parachute. Which force is biggest in each part?

Printed-rocket track (12+): do the same, then read the report's power bar: it's thrust ÷ weight, and it needs 5× or more. Write the number on W2. Keep a straw rocket: it's a handy model on Day 3.
