Day 10

Launch day and mission report

Time: a morning or afternoon outside, plus about an hour for the mission report (this can be the next day). Worksheets: Paper-rocket launch log (everyone), Flight log (12+), and the Capstone planner and rubric.

Objectives

  • Launch rockets safely as a team: paper-track rules for everyone, plus the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code for motor rockets (12+).
  • Track heights with the inclinometer and record every launch in a launch log.
  • Compare what really happened with a prediction, and explain the difference.
  • Present a mission report that uses the science from this unit.

Vocabulary

  • Launch log (or flight log): the record of each launch: the rocket, the launcher or motor, the weather, and what happened.
  • Prediction: what you expect to happen, before the test.
  • Percent difference (12+): how far a measurement is from a prediction, as a percentage.
  • Recovery crew: the people who go and get the rocket, once it's safe.

Plus every word from this unit!

Materials

Launch box: paper track (everyone)

  • Stomp rocket and spare rockets, straw rockets, your best drop-test parachute
  • Fizzy rocket supplies and water (the adult keeps the tablets)
  • Safety glasses for everyone, inclinometers, a tape measure, stopwatches, the paper-rocket launch log on a clipboard, pencils
  • A tablet or phone with Rocket Builder, to show your design's simulated launch
  • Water, snacks, hats, sunscreen

Add for the printed-rocket track (12+)

  • The rocket, parachute packed, plus spare wadding
  • Motors and igniters (a grown-up carries these), and the plugs that come with the igniters
  • Launch pad, rod and blast deflector; the launch controller, safety key (in a grown-up's pocket) and fresh batteries
  • Spare tape, scissors, a small screwdriver or pliers, a rag or sandpaper for the igniter clips
  • A bucket of water or a fire extinguisher, and a spray bottle
  • The flight log (12+ page)

Teach (read aloud before you go)

For everyone (paper rockets):

Read aloud

Today's the day! Everything we learned this week happens in a few seconds.

When you stomp, the air in the bottle rushes up the tube and pushes the rocket off: a push one way, a push back the other way. That's Newton's third law. The push is much bigger than the rocket's weight, so the forces are unbalanced and it zooms up: Newton's second law. The fins keep the nose pointing forward, because the balance point is in front of the push point.

Then the push is over. Drag and weight slow the rocket down as it coasts up. At the very top, apogee, it stops for a moment, then falls back down.

Our trackers measure the angle at the top. Our recorder writes it all down. Afterwards, we'll compare what happened with what we predicted.

Add for the 12+ track (real motor):

Read aloud

A motor works the same way, but much harder and for longer. When the button is pressed, the igniter lights the motor. Hot gas rushes out the nozzle and pushes the rocket up, at more than 5 times its weight. It slides up the rod fast enough for the fins to steer. Then the motor burns out and the rocket coasts, trailing smoke from the delay. Near apogee the ejection charge pops the nose and out comes the parachute. Drag and weight balance, and it drifts down at a gentle, steady speed.

Remember: grown-ups handle the motors, the igniters and the key. Everyone else stays back, watches the rocket, and never chases it into danger.

Launch day rules

Everyone (paper rockets):

  1. Glasses on. Nobody stands in front of or over a launcher.
  2. Count down out loud from 5. Nobody stomps until "launch!"
  3. Never aim at people, pets, windows or roads.
  4. Fizzy rockets: the grown-up runs them. If one doesn't pop, wait 30 seconds; the grown-up picks it up.
  5. Never chase a rocket into a road, a tree or a power line.

Motor rockets (12+), following the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code:

  1. Grown-ups only: motors, igniters, the launch key, loading the pad and clipping the wires.
  2. Everyone wears safety glasses at the pad and stays at least 15 feet (5 m) back when a rocket is armed (30 feet for motors bigger than D).
  3. Wind under 20 mph (less is better). No launching near trees, power lines, buildings, roads or people who aren't part of the launch.
  4. Count down out loud from 5. Everyone watches the rocket until it's on the ground.
  5. Misfire: the grown-up takes out the key and everyone waits 60 seconds before going to the pad.
  6. The recovery crew goes only when the grown-up in charge says so. Never try to catch a rocket.
  7. After the flight, the motor casing is hot. A grown-up takes it out once it cools.

Activity 1 ★ Paper-rocket launch day (everyone)

  1. Predict first. Before each launch, write a guess for how high it will go.
  2. Stomp rockets: two trackers stand 10–15 m from the launcher, on different sides, with their inclinometers. Count down and stomp. Each tracker pinches the string at the top. Try a soft stomp, a medium stomp and a hard stomp, or rockets with different fins or noses.
  3. Parachute rocket: tape your best drop-test parachute into a paper rocket (or fold it into a stomp rocket's nose) and launch it. Does it open? Time how long it takes to come down.
  4. Fizzy rocket contest (adult-led): which amount of water wins?
  5. Your Rocket Builder design: open it and press ▶ Watch it launch. Watch the boost, the coast, the parachute and the drift. Write down its predicted height and compare it with your stomp rockets.

Want to see real rockets too? A local NAR club launch is a great trip: most welcome visitors, and you can watch dozens of rockets fly.

Activity 2 (12+ track) Launch the printed rocket

Grown-up steps for each flight. Follow the motor maker's instructions and your build notes; this is the order:

  1. Check the wind, the field and the area around the pad (no dry grass).
  2. Range safety check: rocket in good shape, nose slides off, shock cord tied, swing-tested on Day 9.
  3. Put flame-resistant wadding into the body, then the packed parachute and the shock cord, then the nose. (The student can help here.)
  4. Grown-up: put the igniter into the motor's nozzle and hold it in with its plug. Put the motor in the rocket and lock the motor retainer.
  5. Grown-up: slide the rocket onto the launch rod. With the key out, clip the wires to the igniter, keeping the clips from touching each other.
  6. Everyone goes back to the controller, at least 15 feet away. Trackers go to their baselines.
  7. Grown-up: puts in the key and checks the light.
  8. Countdown caller: "Range is clear! 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" The grown-up presses and holds the button until it lifts off.
  9. Grown-up: takes out the key right away.
  10. Trackers pinch their inclinometer strings at apogee. Timers time liftoff to parachute and parachute to landing.
  11. The recovery crew goes when the grown-up says so, and notes how far it landed from the pad (count paces).

Activity 3 ★ Launch log and comparison

  1. Fill in the launch log for every launch (paper rockets), and the flight log for every motor flight (12+).
  2. Work out each height: baseline × multiplier + eye height (12+: or baseline × tan of the angle). Average your trackers.
  3. Compare with the prediction: your guess before the launch, or (12+) Rocket Builder's predicted altitude.
  4. Write why they might be different. Ideas: a harder or softer stomp; wind tilted the path; tracking errors (missing the very top). 12+: the real rocket is heavier or lighter than the app thought; rough paint or glue makes more drag; real motors vary a little from one to the next.

Example (paper): a tracker 10 m from the launcher reads 50°. The multiplier for 50° is 1.19, so the height is 10 × 1.19 + 1.3 ≈ 13 m.

Example (12+): predicted 140 m. Trackers read 50° and 54° from a 100 m baseline. Multipliers 1.19 and 1.38, so 119 m and 138 m (plus 1.5 m eye height). Average ≈ 130 m. That's about 10 m lower than predicted.

Capstone project: mission report

Each student makes a mission report: a poster, a short slide show, a written report, or a 3–5 minute talk. Younger kids: a labelled poster and a 2-minute talk is plenty. It must have these parts (the Capstone planner in the worksheets lists them):

  1. Mission: what you set out to do.
  2. Design: a picture of your Rocket Builder rocket (a screenshot or a drawing), with the balance point and push point marked, and its stability score. Why did you choose this design? Use data from your Day 6 log. (Paper track: you can also show your best paper or stomp rocket.)
  3. The science: explain in your own words (a) how Newton's third law makes a rocket go, (b) why the balance point must be in front of the push point, and (c) how a parachute works.
  4. Prediction vs launch: your launch-log numbers next to your predictions, in a table or graph. (12+: the printed rocket's flight vs the app's prediction.)
  5. What happened and why: explain any differences.
  6. Next time: one change you'd make, and what you predict it would do.
  7. Safety: two rules your team followed, and why they matter.

Rubric

Score each row from 1 to 4. Total: 20 points. 17–20 = mission accomplished; 13–16 = solid flight; 9–12 = needs another iteration; below 9 = back to the pad.

Discussion questions

  1. Which part of the flight matched the prediction best? Which matched worst?
  2. Where did you see each of Newton's laws today?
  3. Did the parachute come out near apogee? Was the delay about right?
  4. How far did the rocket drift? Did the wind direction match where it landed?
  5. If you flew again tomorrow, what one thing would you change?

Quick check

  1. In which order do these happen: apogee, liftoff, burnout, landing, parachute opens?
  2. What makes the rocket go up, in terms of Newton's third law?
  3. Two trackers measured a stomp rocket at 12 m and 14 m. What's the average?
  4. A rocket was predicted to fly 150 m and flew 120 m. How many metres short was it? (Grades 7–8: what percent?)
  5. Who takes the spent motor out of the rocket?
Answer key
  1. Liftoff, burnout, apogee, parachute opens, landing.
  2. The motor pushes hot gas down, and the gas pushes the rocket up. (Stomp rocket: the air is pushed up the tube and pushes the rocket off.)
  3. (12 + 14) ÷ 2 = 13 m.
  4. 150 − 120 = 30 m short. Percent difference = 30 ÷ 150 × 100 = 20%.
  5. A grown-up, after it cools.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): be the stomper, the countdown caller or a timer, and do one tracking angle with the multiplier table. The mission report can be a labelled poster and a short talk.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • Use tan and two trackers. Work out the percent difference from the prediction for each flight.
  • Average speed on the way up: altitude ÷ time from liftoff to apogee. Why is it much lower than Rocket Builder's top speed?
  • Descent rate from your timing: altitude ÷ time under the parachute. Compare with the app's descent speed.
  • Make a graph comparing predicted and measured altitudes for every flight (yours, or several from a club launch).

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): compare your stomp-rocket heights with your Rocket Builder design's predicted height. The app's number is for a real motor, so it's much higher. Why? (A motor pushes for a whole second or more; a stomp pushes for a split second.) Want to see real rockets fly? Visit a local NAR club launch as spectators.

Printed-rocket track (12+): first flight on the smallest motor that gets a ✓ in Rocket Builder. If the rocket comes back in good shape, it can fly again with a new motor and fresh wadding. Log every flight.
