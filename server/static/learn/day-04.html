Day 4

Drag and shape

Time: about 75 minutes. Worksheet: W4 Drag test.

Objectives

  • Explain what drag is and list what makes it bigger: speed, size of the front, shape and roughness.
  • Run a fair test comparing nose shapes, with one thing changed at a time.
  • Explain the trade-off with fins: they keep a rocket straight, but they add drag and weight.
  • Make and read a bar graph of averages.

Vocabulary

  • Drag: air pushing back on something moving through it.
  • Streamlined: shaped so air flows smoothly around it, with little drag.
  • Frontal area: the size of the front, as you'd see it looking at the nose.
  • Drag coefficient (Cd): a number for how slippery a shape is. Lower is more slippery. Model rockets are about 0.5 to 0.75.
  • Variable: something that can change in an experiment.
  • Fair test: a test where you change only one variable and keep everything else the same.
  • Trade-off: when making one thing better makes another thing worse.

Materials

Household:

  • Paper, cardstock, tape, scissors, pencils and straws (as on Day 2)
  • Tape measure
  • Optional, for a fairer launch: an empty plastic bottle (500 ml), a straw, modeling clay or tape to seal the straw into the cap
  • Optional: kitchen scale

To buy: nothing new (safety glasses from Day 2).

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

Stick your hand out of a car window (with a grown-up's OK). Hold it flat, and the air slides past. Turn it so your palm faces forward, and the air shoves it back hard. That shove is drag.

Four things make drag bigger:

  1. Speed. Go twice as fast and drag gets about four times bigger. A rocket at full speed feels a lot of drag.
  2. The size of the front. A fat rocket pushes more air out of the way than a thin one.
  3. Shape. A pointed or rounded nose lets air flow around it smoothly. A flat front makes the air crash into it. Scientists give each shape a number called the drag coefficient. Model rockets are usually between 0.5 and 0.75. Rocket Builder uses 0.65 unless you change it.
  4. Roughness and bumps. Rough paint, sharp fin edges and launch lugs all add a bit of drag.

Fins are a trade-off. Fins keep a rocket stable (Day 3), but every fin also adds drag and weight. Rocket designers want fins that are just big enough.

Today we'll be scientists. We'll change one thing at a time and keep everything else the same. That's called a fair test.

Activity 1 ★ Nose-cone test

  1. Build three straw rockets exactly the same: same paper strip, same length, same fins. Leave the tops open.
  2. Give each a different nose: - Flat: put a piece of tape straight across the top, flat. - Cone: roll a small paper cone and tape it on top. - Round: fold and pinch the top into a rounded dome, and tape it smooth.
  3. Make them weigh the same. If you have a scale, add small pieces of tape to the lighter ones. If not, use the same amount of tape on each.
  4. Pick one launch angle (30° or 45°) and keep it the same.
  5. Launch each rocket 3 times. Measure the distance each time.
  6. Fairer launches (optional): make a squeeze launcher. Push a straw through a hole in a bottle cap and seal it with clay. Put the rocket on the straw. Lay the bottle on the floor and stomp on it, or drop a heavy book on it from the same height every time.
  7. Find the averages and make a bar graph.
What to expect

the flat nose usually goes the shortest distance. The cone and round noses are often close; small differences may just be luck. That's why we do 3 trials.

Activity 2: Fin test

  1. Make four rockets that are the same except for the fins: - No fins - 3 small fins (about 2 cm) - 3 medium fins (about 4 cm) - 4 large fins (about 6 cm)
  2. Launch each 3 times at the same angle. Measure the distance. Also watch: does it fly straight or wobble?
What to expect

no fins often wobbles and lands short. Medium fins fly straight. Very large fins fly straight but can lose a little distance from extra drag and weight.

Discussion questions

  1. Which nose went furthest? Was the difference big, or could it be luck?
  2. Why must the rockets weigh the same for the test to be fair?
  3. Why do you think fast real rockets (and race cars, and birds) are long and pointy?
  4. Where do you see the fin trade-off in your results?
  5. What else made drag in your straw rocket? (Tape edges, a bent fin, a crushed nose…)

Quick check

  1. Name three things that make drag bigger.
  2. If a rocket goes twice as fast, how much bigger is the drag?
  3. In a fair test, how many variables do you change at once?
  4. Which has more drag: a flat nose or a pointed nose?
  5. Why not just put huge fins on every rocket?
Answer key
  1. Speed, a bigger front (frontal area), a blunt shape, roughness or bumps. (Any three.)
  2. About four times bigger (drag grows with speed × speed).
  3. One.
  4. A flat nose.
  5. Huge fins add drag and weight, so the rocket doesn't go as high. They can also make it too stable, so it turns into the wind.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): do the nose-cone test only, with 2 trials each. Use the squeeze launcher so every launch gets the same push. Grades 3–4: compare the longest trial for each nose instead of averaging.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • The drag formula: drag = ½ × air density × speed² × Cd × frontal area. Air density is about 1.2 kg/m³.
  • A rocket 30 mm wide has a frontal area of π × 0.015² ≈ 0.00071 m². With Cd = 0.65 at 50 m/s: drag ≈ 0.5 × 1.2 × 2500 × 0.65 × 0.00071 ≈ 0.7 N. That's about the weight of the whole rocket (an 80 g rocket weighs about 0.8 N)! At 25 m/s, half the speed, drag is only about 0.17 N, a quarter as much.
  • In Rocket Builder's Advanced mode, open Flight → Advanced and change the drag coefficient from 0.65 to 0.5, then to 0.8. Build each time and write down the predicted altitude. How much does drag matter?

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): in Rocket Builder, keep everything the same and change only the nose shape (Ogive, Cone, Round, Parabolic, Von Kármán). Build each one and write down the score. The score hardly changes: at model-rocket speeds, the nose shape matters much less than the fins. (The app uses the same drag number for every nose, so the predicted height changes only a little, from the different weights.)

Printed-rocket track (12+): the same, plus the drag-coefficient test in the grade 7–8 extension. A rounded nose (Round or Parabolic) is strong and easy to print. Smooth, sanded fin edges help with drag.
