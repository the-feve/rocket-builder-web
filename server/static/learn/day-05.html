Day 5

Motors, thrust and impulse

Time: about 60 minutes (younger kids) to 90 minutes (12+). Worksheet: W5 Motor codes and impulse.

Objectives

  • Name the parts of a model rocket motor and what each one does.
  • Decode a motor code like C6-5.
  • Work out total impulse: impulse = average thrust × burn time.
  • Older kids: check a rocket's thrust-to-weight ratio (at least 5 to 1).
  • Run a fizzy film-canister rocket experiment and connect it to Newton's third law.

Vocabulary

  • Motor: the part that makes thrust. Model rocket motors are made in factories and used once.
  • Propellant: the fuel that burns to make hot gas.
  • Nozzle: the narrow hole the gas rushes out of.
  • Delay: a slow-burning part after the propellant. It makes a smoke trail while the rocket coasts up.
  • Ejection charge: a small charge at the top of the motor. It pops off the nose and pushes out the parachute.
  • Igniter (starter): a thin wire that gets hot and lights the motor. Only grown-ups handle igniters.
  • Total impulse: the motor's total push, in newton-seconds (N·s). Average thrust × burn time.
  • Impulse class: the letter in the motor code (1/4A, 1/2A, A, B, C, D, E, F).
  • Thrust curve: a graph of thrust against time.
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio: average thrust ÷ the rocket's weight.

Materials

Household:

  • Water, a measuring spoon or small cup, a stopwatch
  • Paper, tape, scissors (to dress up the canisters as rockets, optional)
  • A wall or fence outside, with tape marks every 50 cm up to 3 m (or a phone to film against the marks)
  • Calculator

To buy:

  • Film canisters with lids that snap inside the rim (10-pack, $8–10). Lids that close over the outside don't seal well enough.
  • Effervescent tablets (Alka-Seltzer or plain fizzy vitamin C tablets, $5–8)
  • Safety glasses for everyone (from Day 2)

12+ track: if your motors have arrived, a grown-up can show one (still in its pack). Don't take it apart. Under 12: no real motors; use the picture on W5.

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

Today we look at the engine of a model rocket: the motor.

A model rocket motor is a small paper tube packed by a factory. From the bottom up, it has:

  1. A nozzle: a narrow clay hole at the bottom. Hot gas rushes out of it.
  2. Propellant: the fuel. It burns for about a second and makes the thrust.
  3. A delay: it burns slowly for a few seconds and makes a smoke trail. No thrust now: the rocket is coasting up.
  4. An ejection charge: a small puff at the top. It pops off the nose and pushes the parachute out, near the top of the flight.

A grown-up puts a thin wire called an igniter into the nozzle. A launch controller sends electricity through it, it gets hot, and it lights the motor. Only grown-ups handle motors and igniters. Always.

Every motor has a code printed on it, like C6-5:

  • C is the impulse class: the total push. Each letter is up to twice the one before. A C motor has up to twice the total push of a B, and up to four times an A.
  • 6 is the average thrust in newtons. (Real measured motors are often a bit off from this rounded number.)
  • 5 is the delay in seconds, from when the propellant burns out to when the parachute pops out.

Total impulse is the average thrust times how long it pushes. A motor pushing 5 N for 2 seconds has 5 × 2 = 10 newton-seconds. A strong push for a short time can equal a gentle push for a long time.

Motors also come in sizes: M13, M18, M24 and M29. The number is the width in millimetres. Rocket Builder makes the motor tube to fit the size you pick.

Bigger isn't always better. A rocket must push at least 5 times its own weight, so it zips off the pad. It must leave the launch rod at 15 m/s or faster, so the fins can steer. And bigger motors need much bigger fields.

Activity 1 ★ Fizzy film-canister rockets (adult-led)

The tablet has two powders in it (an acid and baking soda). When they get wet, they make carbon dioxide gas. The gas fills the canister and the pressure builds until the lid pops. Gas and water shoot down, and the canister shoots up: Newton's third law!

Safety

an adult runs this activity, for every age. Outside, safety glasses on everyone. The adult keeps the tablets and hands out the pieces (Alka-Seltzer contains aspirin, so it's medicine, not candy; plain fizzy vitamin C tablets work too). Never lean over a canister. If one doesn't pop, wait at least 30 seconds; then the adult picks it up, pointing it away from faces.

Experiment: how much water?

  1. Decide what you'll change: the amount of water. Keep the tablet size the same (half a tablet each time).
  2. Make a prediction: which amount will fly highest?
  3. Put 1 teaspoon (5 ml) of water in the canister.
  4. The adult drops in half a tablet and snaps the lid on quickly, then sets it lid-down next to the marked wall. Everyone steps back at least 2 m. Start the stopwatch.
  5. Record the time until it pops and how high it goes (use the wall marks or film it).
  6. Do 3 trials. Then try 2 teaspoons and 3 teaspoons (don't fill more than about half way).
  7. Optional: tape a paper tube, nose cone and fins around the canister (lid end down) to make it look like a rocket. Does it fly differently?
What to expect

heights are usually 1–3 m. The pop time and height change with the amount of water; your data will tell you which works best. Warm water fizzes faster, so it usually pops sooner.

Activity 2: Decode the motors (worksheet)

Work through W5: decode motor codes, calculate impulse, find the class, and (older kids) check thrust-to-weight. This is reading and math only: nobody handles a motor.

Everyone: in Rocket Builder, build your rocket and find the How high will it go? card: the best motor, and how high it should fly. 12+: also open Grown-up details. The motor table lists every motor for your size, with its peak altitude, speed off the rod, thrust × weight, and a ✓ for motors that pass. Copy three rows onto W5.

Discussion questions

  1. How is the fizzy rocket like a real motor? How is it different?
  2. Why is it useful that each class letter is about double the one before?
  3. Why do model rockets need a delay before the parachute comes out?
  4. Why do you think bigger motors need bigger fields?
  5. Which would you pick for a first flight: the biggest motor that passes, or the smallest? Why?

Quick check

  1. In the code B6-4, what do the B, the 6 and the 4 mean?
  2. A motor pushes an average of 5 N for 2 seconds. What's its total impulse? Which class is it?
  3. Which has more total push: a C motor or a B motor? About how much more?
  4. A rocket with its motor weighs 1.2 N. The motor's average thrust is 5 N. Is the thrust at least 5 times the weight?
  5. Who puts the igniter in the motor?
Answer key
  1. B = impulse class (2.51–5 N·s of total push); 6 = average thrust about 6 N; 4 = a 4-second delay before the ejection charge.
  2. 5 × 2 = 10 N·s: a C motor (5.01–10 N·s).
  3. A C motor: up to about twice as much.
  4. No. 5 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 4.2, which is less than 5. It's too heavy for this motor; it would leave the pad too slowly.
  5. A grown-up. Always.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): do the fizzy rocket experiment with an adult, and the decoding part of W5. Grades 3–4: decode the letter and the delay only, and do the impulse problems that use whole numbers (like 5 × 2). Skip thrust-to-weight.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • Impulse from a thrust curve. W5 has a thrust-curve table for a B motor. Plot it, then find the area under the curve by counting squares (or splitting it into triangles and rectangles). The area is the total impulse: about 4.3 N·s, a B motor.
  • F = m × a at liftoff. A printed rocket plus a C6 motor has a mass of 0.086 kg, so it weighs about 0.086 × 9.8 ≈ 0.84 N. The motor's measured average thrust is about 4.7 N. Net force = 4.7 − 0.84 ≈ 3.9 N. Acceleration a = F ÷ m ≈ 3.9 ÷ 0.086 ≈ 45 m/s², more than 4 times gravity. Thrust-to-weight = 4.7 ÷ 0.84 ≈ 5.6, which passes (5 or more).
  • Speed off the rod. If it averaged about 45 m/s² for a 0.9 m rod, how fast would it be going at the top? Use v = √(2 × a × d) = √(2 × 45 × 0.9) ≈ 9 m/s. Rocket Builder says about 16 m/s for this rocket. Why the difference? (The motor's thrust peaks at about 14 N near the start, three times its average, and the rocket is only on the rod for the first 0.16 seconds, right when the push is strongest.)

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): the fizzy rocket is your "motor" for the week, always with an adult and safety glasses. You'll learn how real motors work from the picture on W5 and from the app, but you won't handle one: they're rated 12+. In Rocket Builder, the How high will it go? card shows the best motor for your design and how high it should fly.

Printed-rocket track (12+): don't open or test real motors today. A grown-up can show you the pack: find the code, the delay and the warnings. Motors are stored in a cool, dry place, away from flames and out of reach of younger children.
