Day 6

The engineering design cycle with Rocket Builder

Time: about 90 minutes. Worksheet: W6 Design log.

Objectives

  • Use the engineering design cycle: ask, imagine, plan, create, test, improve.
  • Write the criteria (what a good design must do) and constraints (the limits) for a rocket.
  • Change one thing at a time in Rocket Builder and record what happens to the stability score.
  • Make a line graph and describe the trend.
  • Choose a final design using data, and explain why.

Vocabulary

  • Engineer: someone who designs things to solve problems.
  • Design cycle: the steps engineers repeat: ask, imagine, plan, create, test, improve.
  • Criteria: what the design must do to succeed.
  • Constraints: the limits you have to work within (size, money, time, space).
  • Iteration: one trip around the cycle. Engineers do many.
  • Prototype: a first version, made to test.
  • Trend: the general direction of the data (goes up, goes down, stays flat).
  • Model: something that stands in for the real thing so we can test it. Rocket Builder is a computer model.

Materials

  • A computer or tablet with Rocket Builder (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net)
  • Pencil, ruler, W6 worksheet
  • 12+ track: the 3D printer and filament

To buy: nothing.

Teach (read aloud or adapt)

Read aloud

Engineers almost never get a design right the first time. They follow a loop called the design cycle:

  1. Ask: what's the problem? What must the design do? What are the limits?
  2. Imagine: think of lots of ideas.
  3. Plan: pick one and draw it.
  4. Create: build it, or build a model of it.
  5. Test: does it work? Measure!
  6. Improve: change something and go round again.

Building real rockets to test every idea would take a long time and cost a lot. So engineers use computer models first. Rocket Builder is our model. It works out the balance point, the push point, the weight and how high the rocket should fly, in a few seconds.

Today you'll be a rocket engineer. Here are our criteria, the things a good design must do:

  • Stability score between 1 and 2 cal (2 to 3 is OK too).
  • At least one motor gets a ✓ Good: it pushes 5 times its weight or more, leaves the rod at 15 m/s or faster, and goes at least 30 m high.
  • The predicted height fits the field we can use.

And our constraints, the limits:

  • It must fit our printer (or the parts are split into pieces that do).
  • The field we have is only so big.
  • Motors cost money, so the smallest motor that works is best for a first flight.

The most important rule for testing: change only one thing at a time. If you change the height and the fins together, you won't know which one made the difference.

Activity ★ The Rocket Builder design challenge

Set up

  1. Open Rocket Builder in Beginner mode. Pick a motor size. (M18 is a good choice: the common 18 mm size.)
  2. Set your printer's height. No printer? Leave the default.
  3. Pick a fin shape and a nose shape. Keep these the same for Part A.

Part A: change the height

  1. Set the height to the shortest the app allows. Press Build my rocket.
  2. On the W6 log, write: height, fin shape, stability score, best motor, predicted height (altitude), power (thrust ÷ weight) and launch speed.
  3. Open Grown-up details and look in the Build notes. If it says the fins were enlarged, put a ✓ in the "fins grew" column. (The app makes the fins bigger by itself when a rocket is too wobbly.)
  4. Make the rocket about 50 mm taller. Build again and record.
  5. Repeat until you have at least 5 heights.
  6. Graph it on W6: height along the bottom, score up the side. Join the dots.

Part B: change the fins

  1. Pick one height from Part A. Keep it the same.
  2. Try each fin shape. Build each one and record the score, and whether the fins grew.

Part C: choose

  1. Look at your log. Which designs meet all the criteria?
  2. Fill in the decision table on W6: give each of your top 3 designs a score from 1 to 3 for stability, height (fits our field?), looks and printing time.
  3. Pick a winner. Write one sentence saying why, using your data.
  4. Press Save (and give it a name!). The app keeps your rockets under My rockets.
  5. Press ▶ Watch it launch and watch your winner fly.
What to expect

with fins kept the same size, a taller rocket usually gets a higher score, because the long body moves the balance point forward, away from the heavy motor. Very short rockets often show "fins grew" and a score just above 1, because the app enlarged the fins to make them stable. Swept-back fins push the push point further back than a rectangle of the same size, so they usually score higher. Taller rockets also weigh more, so with the same motor they don't fly quite as high.

Discussion questions

  1. What was the trend in Part A? Did it ever stop going up?
  2. Why does the app check the score with the heaviest motor?
  3. Did any design fail a criterion? Which one, and how could you fix it?
  4. Why is it smart to test on a computer model before printing?
  5. What can't a computer model tell you? (Real weights, print quality, wind, glue…)

Quick check

  1. Put these in order: test, ask, create, improve, imagine, plan.
  2. What's the difference between a criterion and a constraint?
  3. Why change only one thing at a time?
  4. Your design scores 0.8 cal. Name two things you could change.
  5. Your rocket scores 3.6 cal. Is that a problem?
Answer key
  1. Ask, imagine, plan, create, test, improve.
  2. A criterion is what the design must do (be stable, go high enough). A constraint is a limit you must work within (printer size, field size, money).
  3. So you know which change caused the result.
  4. Make the rocket taller; make the fins bigger (or a swept fin shape); use a lighter motor; add weight in the nose. (Any two.)
  5. Yes, a small one: it's over-stable, so it will turn into the wind. Smaller fins or a shorter body would help.

Differentiation

Younger kids (grades 3–6): do Part A with 4 heights, then pick a favourite design that meets the criteria. A grown-up can read the Build notes. Then press ▶ Watch it launch and watch your design fly.

Grade 7–8 extension:

  • Part D: custom fins (Advanced mode). Switch to Advanced. Under Fins, tick Custom fin size. Keep everything else the same and change only the Span (how far the fins stick out): try 15, 20, 25 and 30 mm. Graph score against span. Find the span where the score crosses 1.0. (In one test with an M18 rocket, body length 250 mm and trapezoid fins: span 15 mm gave −0.5 cal, 25 mm gave 1.3 cal and 30 mm gave 1.7 cal. A negative score means the push point is in front of the balance point.)
  • Find the score yourself: in the full report, write down the CG and CP distances and the body width (Key dimensions), then check the score: (CP − CG) ÷ width.
  • Graph a second trend: height vs predicted altitude with the same motor. Why does it go down?

Track notes

Paper track (under 12): your design stays on screen. Press Save, give it a name, and press ▶ Watch it launch to see its flight, its parachute and where it lands. Then make a paper prototype: draw it full size on paper and build a paper-towel-tube model with the same fin shape, for a swing test. Don't download, print or order it: motor rockets are rated 12+.

Printed-rocket track (12+): when your design is picked and it shows Download STLs, download the ZIP. A grown-up starts the first print today. The build notes and the parts list tell you each part's print height. Print the parts in the order the build notes give; a long rocket is split into several sections. Some prints take several hours, so plan around them. No printer? Rocket Builder can order a printed rocket for you (a grown-up needs an account).
