Lesson 8 Grades 3–5

Launch day: how high did it go?

Time: about 45–60 minutes outside. Worksheets: E8 Launch log, the 45° sighting card, and the Rocket Scientist certificate. Rocket Builder: on a tablet or projector. Optional instructor demo: a 3D-printed rocket.

4-PS3-1 Explain that a faster object has more energy.
3-5-ETS1-3 Test a model and decide what to improve.

Practice: planning and carrying out investigations; constructing explanations. Crosscutting concepts: patterns; energy and matter.

I can…

  • I can follow the launch rules and do my launch-team job.
  • I can use a 45° sighting card to tell if a rocket went higher than my distance from the launcher.
  • I can explain that a harder stomp makes the rocket go faster, so it has more energy and goes higher.

Words to know

  • Energy: what makes things move. A faster rocket has more energy.
  • Tracker: the person who watches the rocket to measure how high it goes.
  • Countdown: "5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" Everyone knows when it will go.

Materials

  • Improved stomp rockets (Lesson 6) and the parachute rocket (Lesson 7)
  • The stomp launcher, cones, safety glasses for everyone
  • 45° sighting cards (on the worksheets; cut out and fold), a tape measure
  • Worksheet E8 on a clipboard, pencils
  • A tablet or projector with Rocket Builder

Steps

1 Before you go out (10 minutes)

Read the launch rules together (see the safety page). Give out jobs: countdown caller, stomper, two trackers, recorder, recovery crew. Everyone takes a turn.

How the 45° card works: fold a square of paper corner to corner to make a triangle. Hold the long edge level and sight up the slanted edge. That line goes up at 45°. If you stand 10 m from the launcher and the rocket goes above your 45° line, it went higher than 10 m. Below the line? Lower than 10 m.

2 Launch! (25 minutes)

  1. Trackers stand 10 m from the launcher (measure it), off to the side.
  2. Predict: before each launch, the recorder writes a guess. Higher or lower than the 45° line?
  3. Countdown, stomp. Trackers call "above!" or "below!"
  4. Try a soft, a medium and a hard stomp with the same rocket. Record each.
  5. Fly the parachute rocket. Does the parachute open?

3 Rocket Builder and the real thing (5 minutes)

On the tablet or projector, open a Rocket Builder design and press ▶ Watch it launch (Simple mode on a projector). Read the predicted height. Compare: the app's rocket flies on a real motor, which pushes for about a second. A stomp pushes for a split second. That's why the app's rocket goes much higher.

4 Wrap up (10 minutes)

  • What pattern did you see between the stomp and the height? (Harder stomp, faster rocket, higher flight.)
  • Tell a partner the story of your best flight, in order: before, during, after.
  • Hand out the Rocket Scientist certificates.

Check for understanding

  1. A rocket goes above the 45° line. The tracker stood 10 m away. Did it go higher or lower than 10 m?
  2. A hard stomp sends the rocket higher than a soft stomp. Which rocket was moving faster when it left the launcher? Which had more energy?
  3. Why do we count down out loud?
Answer key
  1. Higher than 10 m.
  2. The hard-stomp rocket: it was faster, so it had more energy.
  3. So everyone knows exactly when the rocket will launch and is watching and ready.

Teacher notes

  • Safety: glasses on. Nobody stands in front of or over the launcher. Stomp only on "launch!" Never chase a rocket into a road, tree or power line.
  • Grade 3: use only "above or below the line". Grade 5: use a protractor inclinometer (from the middle-school worksheets) and the shortcut: at 30° the height is about half the distance; at 45° it equals the distance; at 60° it's about 1.7 times the distance. Add about 1.3 m for eye height.
  • Optional instructor demo, a printed rocket on a real motor: only if an adult experienced with model rockets runs it, following the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code, on a field big enough for the motor (see the middle-school safety page), with students behind a spectator line at least 30 feet (10 m) from the pad. Students never handle motors, igniters or the launch key, and never go to the pad. A simpler option: watch a local NAR club launch as spectators, or show a video of one. Kits and motor flights are rated 12+.
  • Rain or wind? Do launch day in a gym with straw rockets, and use Rocket Builder's simulated launch.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free Rocketry Reservoir (apogeerockets.com/downloads/PDFs/Rocketry_Education_Pack.pdf) has rocket colouring pages and certificates you can print. The NAR (nar.org) lists clubs that welcome school groups at their launches.
  • Rocket Builder tools for launch day: the altitude calculator (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net/tools/altitude) turns an angle and a distance into a height, shows the working, the 45° rule and the average of two trackers (good for grade 5). Each design also has a one-page mission card (designer ▾ download menu → Mission card) with its predicted height, to print for the clipboard. The simulator's Predict, fly, compare log keeps the class's guesses and results.
