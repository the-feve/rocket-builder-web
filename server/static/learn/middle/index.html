Rockets: middle school track

Nine lessons, about 55–60 minutes each, plus a launch day. Built on the Washington State K–12 Science Learning Standards (the NGSS). Goes with Rocket Builder by Quiver Design Works (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net), in Beginner mode first and Advanced mode from Lesson 6.

Students investigate forces, stability, drag, motors and energy with paper rockets, then design a rocket in Rocket Builder, 3D-print it (or order it printed) and fly it on a real model rocket motor. Adults handle all motors and igniters, every time, under the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code. Motor flights and kits are rated 12+: students under 12 (some 6th graders) do every lesson and take launch-day jobs (tracker, recorder, countdown caller), and keep their designs on screen.

Rocket Builder: Beginner, then Advanced

  • Lessons 1–5, Beginner: the tutorial walks through motor size, printer height, rocket height, fins and nose. Students read the stability score, the blue (CG) and red (CP) dots, the power bar, How high will it go? and Which parachute?, and press ▶ Watch it launch.
  • Lesson 6 on, Advanced: custom fin size, drag coefficient, wall thickness and the full report with key dimensions (CG and CP distances). The motor table under Grown-up details lists every motor for the size with a ✓ for those that pass.
  • Saved designs keep their link; an account (adult, or 13+) keeps them across devices.

A note on the numbers

They match Rocket Builder: stability 1–2 cal is ideal, under 1 is too wobbly to download, over 3 is over-stable; thrust at least 5 times the weight; at least 15 m/s off the rod; at least 30 m high so the parachute can open; a parachute that brings the rocket down at about 3–5 m/s. The app's numbers are estimates. Comparing them with the real flight is the point of Lesson 9.

Worksheets

The middle-school worksheets (US Letter): W1–W9, the inclinometer template, safety stories and launch checklists, the flight log, the mission report planner and rubric, quick checks and answer keys. Print at 100%.
