Lesson 2 Grades 6–8

The forces in a rocket flight

Time: about 55 minutes. Worksheet: W2 Forces and launch angles. Rocket Builder: Beginner mode.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-PS2-2 A change in motion depends on the sum of the forces and the mass.
MS-PS2-4 (connection) Gravity pulls masses together; a rocket's weight is Earth's pull on its mass.
6.RP.A.3 (math) Ratio reasoning: thrust-to-weight.

Practice: developing and using models (force diagrams). Crosscutting concept: systems and system models.

I can…

  • I can draw force diagrams for each part of a flight: on the pad, boost, coast, parachute.
  • I can tell balanced from unbalanced forces and say what each does to motion.
  • I can check a thrust-to-weight ratio of at least 5 to 1.

Words to know

  • Thrust, weight, drag, lift: the four forces. Weight always points down; drag points opposite to the motion; fins make lift that steers the nose back into line.
  • Net force: what's left when you add all the forces.
  • Apogee: the highest point of the flight.
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio: thrust ÷ weight.

Materials

Paper strips (8 × 20 cm), pencils, straws, cardstock, tape, scissors, a protractor taped to a box, a tape measure, safety glasses, W2.

Steps

1. Teach (10 minutes)

Read aloud

On the pad, the weight pulls down and the pad pushes up: balanced, so nothing changes. At liftoff the thrust is far bigger than weight plus drag, so the rocket accelerates. Model rockets need thrust at least 5 times their weight, so they leave the rod fast enough for the fins to steer. After burnout there's no thrust; weight and drag both slow it as it coasts to apogee. Under the parachute, drag up equals weight down: balanced again, so it falls at a steady speed.

2. Model it (10 minutes)

On W2, draw and label the forces in the four flight pictures. Longer arrow = bigger force. Mark which pictures are balanced.

3. Investigate: straw rockets and launch angle (25 minutes)

  1. Roll a paper tube around a pencil, tape it, close the nose airtight, add 3 small fins.
  2. Glasses on. Launch at 15°, 30°, 45°, 60° and 75°, 2–3 trials each, the same breath each time (or a squeeze bottle).
  3. Record and graph mean distance against angle.

4. Rocket Builder (5 minutes)

▶ Watch it launch: name each phase aloud. In the report, read the power bar (thrust ÷ weight). Is it 5× or more?

5. Wrap up (5 minutes)

Why does the rocket keep going up after the motor stops? (Inertia: it's moving up; weight and drag slow it, they don't stop it at once.)

Check for understanding

  1. During the coast, which forces act on the rocket?
  2. Thrust 6 N, weight 1 N, drag 0.5 N. What's the net force, and which way?
  3. Is thrust 6 N on a 1.2 N rocket at least 5 times the weight?
  4. Under a parachute at steady speed, are the forces balanced?
Answer key
  1. Weight and drag (no thrust).
  2. 6 − 1 − 0.5 = 4.5 N up.
  3. 6 ÷ 1.2 = 5: just passes.
  4. Yes: drag up equals weight down.

Teacher notes

  • What to expect: about 40–45° usually goes farthest. Real model rockets launch nearly straight up so they land inside the field.
  • Extension: net force 4.5 N on a 0.1 kg rocket: a = 45 m/s², about 4½ times gravity. Draw arrows to scale (1 cm = 1 N).
  • About MS-PS2-4: this lesson only connects to it (weight = gravity on mass); the full PE needs an argument about masses and gravitational attraction.
  • Safety: glasses on for everyone; never aim at people; fetch only on "clear".
