# Day 8: Measuring height with angles

[← Day 7](day-07.md) · [Overview](README.md) · [Day 9 →](day-09.md)

**Time:** about 90 minutes. **Worksheet:** W8 Altitude tracking, plus the **inclinometer template**.

## Objectives

- Build an inclinometer (an angle-measurer) and use it to read an angle of elevation.
- Find the height of something tall using a measured distance and an angle.
- Use a table of multipliers (grade 5–6) or the tangent (grade 7–8) to calculate height.
- Explain where errors come from and how to reduce them.

## Vocabulary

- **Altitude**: height above the ground.
- **Inclinometer**: a tool that measures how steeply you're looking up.
- **Angle of elevation**: the angle between looking straight ahead (level) and looking up at something.
- **Baseline**: the measured distance along the ground from you to the spot under the object.
- **Right triangle**: a triangle with one square corner (90°).
- **Tangent (tan)**: for a right triangle, height ÷ baseline. Each angle has its own tangent number.
- **Error**: the difference between a measurement and the true value.

## Materials

Household:

- The inclinometer template (printed from `worksheets.html` at 100% size)
- Cardstock or a cereal box, glue stick, scissors
- A drinking straw, tape
- About 25 cm of thread or thin string, and a small weight (a washer, nut or paper clip)
- A tape measure (the longer the better) or a string marked every metre
- A calculator
- Something tall to measure: a tree, a flagpole, a house, a light pole

To buy: nothing. (Optional: a stomp rocket, $15–25, or a DIY one, to practise tracking.)

## Teach (read aloud or adapt)

> How do you find out how high a rocket went? You can't hold a tape measure up 100 metres! But you can measure an **angle**.
>
> Stand a measured distance from the launch pad. That's your **baseline**. When the rocket reaches the top, look up at it and measure how steeply you're looking. That's the **angle of elevation**.
>
> Now picture a giant triangle. One side runs along the ground from you to the pad. One side goes straight up from the pad to the rocket. And your line of sight goes from your eye to the rocket. The corner at the pad is a square corner. That makes a **right triangle**.
>
> If you know the baseline and the angle, the triangle can only be one size. So you can work out the height!
>
> Here's an easy one: if the angle is **45°**, the height is the **same as** the baseline. Stand 50 m away, look up at 45°, and the rocket is 50 m up.
>
> For other angles, use the **multiplier table** on your worksheet: **height = baseline × multiplier**. Mathematicians call that multiplier the **tangent** of the angle.
>
> One more thing: your eyes aren't on the ground. Add your eye height (about 1.3 m for a kid, 1.6 m for a grown-up) to get the full height.
>
> **Never look at the sun** through your inclinometer straw.

## Activity 1 ★ Build an inclinometer

1. Print the template at **100% size** (check the 10 cm test bar with a ruler).
2. Glue it onto cardstock and cut it out.
3. Tape a straw along the top edge, exactly along the line marked "straw".
4. Poke a small hole at the dot marked "pivot".
5. Push the thread through the hole and tape or knot it at the back. Tie the weight to the other end, so it hangs below the lowest marks.
6. Test it: hold it level (look through the straw at something at your own eye height). The string should hang on **0°**.

**How to use it:** look through the straw at the top of the object. Let the string settle, then pinch it against the card with a finger. Read the angle where the string crosses the scale. A partner can read it for you.

## Activity 2 ★ How tall is that tree?

1. Pick something tall. Measure a baseline: walk straight back from its base, **20 m** is a good start. Write it down.
2. Look through the straw at the very top. Read the angle. Do it 3 times and average.
3. Find the multiplier for your angle in the table. Height = baseline × multiplier + your eye height.
4. Move to a different baseline (say 30 m) and measure again. Do you get about the same height?

**Example:** baseline 20 m, angle 35°. Multiplier for 35° is 0.70. Height = 20 × 0.70 + 1.3 = 14 + 1.3 = **15.3 m**.

## Activity 3 (optional): Track a stomp rocket

1. Put on safety glasses. Set up the stomp rocket pointing straight up.
2. Trackers stand 10–15 m from the launcher, off to the side, with their inclinometers.
3. Count down "5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" and stomp.
4. Each tracker follows the rocket and pinches the string at the highest point.
5. Work out the height. If two trackers stand on different sides, average their answers.

## Tracking a real rocket on Day 10

- Rockets go much higher, so stand further away: a baseline about **half to all** of the predicted altitude gives good angles (between about 30° and 60°).
- Stand to the **side of the wind**, not upwind or downwind, so drift doesn't change your distance much.
- Watch for the moment the rocket stops climbing (the smoke trail curves over, and the parachute often pops out just after).

## Discussion questions

1. Why is 45° a "magic" angle?
2. Why should you stand further away for a higher rocket?
3. Name two things that could make your height measurement wrong.
4. Why does it help to have two trackers?
5. The method assumes the rocket goes straight up over the pad. What happens if the wind tilts its path?

## Quick check

1. You stand 30 m from a flagpole and look up at 45°. How tall is it (ignore eye height)?
2. Baseline 50 m, angle 60° (multiplier 1.73). How high is the rocket?
3. Which side of the triangle is the baseline?
4. Why add your eye height?
5. Should you ever look at the sun through the straw?

**Answer key**

1. **30 m** (at 45°, height = baseline).
2. 50 × 1.73 ≈ **87 m** (plus eye height, about 88–89 m).
3. The side along the ground, from you to the spot under the object.
4. Because you measure the angle from your eye, not from the ground.
5. Never.

## Differentiation

**Grade 5:** use the multiplier table and a calculator. Or skip the math and make a **scale drawing**: on graph paper, let 1 square = 2 m. Draw the baseline, use a protractor to draw your angle, draw a line straight up from the far end, and count squares to find the height.

**Grade 8 extension:**

- Use the tangent: **height = baseline × tan(angle) + eye height**. Use a calculator's tan button (in degree mode).
- **Error check:** if your angle is off by 2°, how much does the height change at 45° and at 75°? With a 50 m baseline: tan 45° = 1.00 and tan 47° = 1.07, so 50 m vs 53.6 m (about 7% off). tan 75° = 3.73 and tan 77° = 4.33, so 187 m vs 217 m (about 16% off). Steep angles magnify errors. That's why a long baseline is better for high flights.
- **Two trackers:** why can averaging two trackers on opposite sides cancel out some of the error from wind tilt?
