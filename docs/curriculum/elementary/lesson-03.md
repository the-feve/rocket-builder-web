# Lesson 3: Find the pattern: launch angles

**Time:** about 45 minutes. **Worksheet:** E3 Launch angles.

## Standards

| Code | In plain words |
|---|---|
| **3-PS2-2** | Measure how something moves, find a pattern, and use it to predict what happens next. |
| **4.MD.C.6** (math) | Measure angles with a protractor. |
| **3.MD.B.3** (math) | Show results on a bar graph. |

**Practice:** planning and carrying out investigations. **Crosscutting concept:** patterns.

## I can…

- I can set a launch angle with a protractor.
- I can measure how far my rocket goes and record it.
- I can find a pattern in my results and use it to predict a new angle.

## Words to know

- **Angle**: how much something is tilted. We measure it in **degrees** (°).
- **Pattern**: something that happens the same way again and again.
- **Predict**: say what you think will happen, using what you already know.

## Materials

For each group:

- Straw rockets from Lesson 2 (or new ones)
- A squeeze launcher: an empty plastic bottle with a straw pushed through a hole in the cap, sealed with modeling clay
- A protractor taped to the edge of a box or a book
- A tape measure, sticky notes to mark landing spots
- **Safety glasses**
- Worksheet E3

## Steps

### 1. Hook (5 minutes)

Ask: *If you want to throw a ball as far as you can, do you throw it flat, straight up, or in between?* Let students show with their arm.

### 2. Learn (5 minutes)

> Scientists look for **patterns**. If something happens the same way each time, we can **predict** what will happen next. Today we'll test which tilt, or **angle**, sends a rocket farthest, and look for the pattern.

### 3. Do: the angle test (25 minutes)

<!-- figure: squeeze-launcher -->

1. Put the rocket on the bottle's straw. Hold the straw along the protractor at **30°**.
2. Squeeze the bottle hard, the same way each time. Mark where the rocket lands. Measure the distance.
3. Do **2 tries** at 30°, then 2 at **45°**, then 2 at **60°**.
4. Colour a bar for the farthest try at each angle on E3.
5. **Predict:** look at your bars. Will **75°** go farther or shorter than 60°? Write your guess, then test it.

**Fair test:** the same rocket, the same launcher, the same squeeze. Only the angle changes.

### 4. Wrap up (10 minutes)

<!-- figure: angle-paths -->

- What pattern did you see? (Going from 30° up to about 45°, the rocket went farther; steeper than that, it went shorter.)
- Was your 75° prediction right? How did the pattern help you guess?

## Check for understanding

1. What one thing did we change in our test?
2. Which angle went farthest for most groups?
3. Finish the sentence: "If I launch at 80°, I predict it will go ______, because ______."

**Answer key**

1. The launch angle.
2. Usually around 45° (often a bit less, about 40°).
3. Shorter, because past about 45° the rockets went shorter each time we tilted up more (it goes high but not far).

## Teacher notes

- **Make the squeeze launcher before class.** A 500 ml bottle and clay to seal the straw works well. For an even push, drop a heavy book on the bottle from the same height each time, or have the same student squeeze every time.
- **Real rockets fly almost straight up**, not at 45°, so they land close by, inside the field, away from people. Point this out so no one thinks 45° is how real rockets launch.
- **Grade 3:** use only 30°, 45° and 60°, and compare by marking the landing spots with sticky notes (no measuring). **Grade 5:** do 3 tries and find the average.
- **Protractor help:** many students read the wrong scale. Show them which zero to start from.
- **Safety:** glasses on. Launch in one direction only, with nobody downrange. Students fetch rockets only when you say "clear".
- **Rocket Builder link:** the launch simulator has a launch-angle setting (0–30°). Show 0° and 30° on the projector: real rockets launch close to straight up so they land near the pad.
