# Day 10: Launch day and mission report

[← Day 9](day-09.md) · [Overview](README.md)

**Time:** a half day at the field, plus about an hour for the mission report (this can be the next day). **Worksheet:** Flight log, and the Capstone planner and rubric.

## Objectives

- Fly rockets safely as a launch team, following the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code.
- Track altitude with the inclinometer and record every flight in a flight log.
- Compare what really happened with the prediction, and explain the difference.
- Present a mission report that uses the science from this unit.

## Vocabulary

- **Flight log**: the record of each flight: the rocket, the motor, the weather, and what happened.
- **Prediction**: what you expect to happen, before the test.
- **Percent difference**: how far a measurement is from a prediction, as a percentage.
- **Recovery crew**: the people who go and get the rocket, once it's safe.

Plus every word from this unit!

## Materials

**Launch box (printer track)**

- The rocket, parachute packed, plus spare wadding
- Motors and igniters (**a grown-up carries these**), and the plugs that come with the igniters
- Launch pad, rod and blast deflector; the launch controller, **safety key** (in a grown-up's pocket) and fresh batteries
- Spare tape, scissors, a small screwdriver or pliers, a rag or sandpaper for the igniter clips
- A bucket of water or a fire extinguisher, and a spray bottle
- Safety glasses, inclinometers, a tape measure, stopwatches, the flight log on a clipboard, pencils
- Water, snacks, hats, sunscreen

**Launch box (paper track)**

- Stomp rocket (and spare rockets), straw rockets, fizzy rocket supplies and water
- Safety glasses, inclinometers, tape measure, stopwatches, the flight log

## Teach (read aloud before you go)

> Today's the day! Everything we learned this week happens in about ten seconds.
>
> When the button is pressed, the igniter lights the motor. Hot gas rushes out the nozzle, and the gas pushes the rocket up: **Newton's third law**. The thrust is more than 5 times the weight, so the forces are **unbalanced** and it leaps off the pad: **Newton's second law**. It slides up the rod fast enough for the fins to steer. If it tilts, the air pushes on the fins behind the **balance point** and swings the nose back. It's **stable**.
>
> Then the motor burns out. **Drag** and **weight** slow it down as it **coasts**, trailing smoke from the **delay**. At the top, **apogee**, the ejection charge pops the nose and out comes the **parachute**. Drag and weight balance, and it drifts down at a gentle, steady speed.
>
> Our trackers measure the angle at apogee. Our recorder writes it all down. And afterwards, we'll compare what happened with what we predicted.
>
> Remember: grown-ups handle the motors, the igniters and the key. Everyone else stays back, watches the rocket, and never chases it into danger.

## Launch day rules

1. **Grown-ups only:** motors, igniters, the launch key, loading the pad and clipping the wires.
2. Everyone wears safety glasses at the pad and stays **at least 15 feet (5 m) back** when a rocket is armed (30 feet for motors bigger than D).
3. Wind under 20 mph (less is better). No launching near trees, power lines, buildings, roads or people who aren't part of the launch.
4. Count down out loud from 5. Everyone watches the rocket until it's on the ground.
5. **Misfire:** the grown-up takes out the key and everyone waits 60 seconds before going to the pad.
6. The recovery crew goes **only** when the grown-up in charge says so. Never chase a rocket into a road, a tree or a power line, and never try to catch it.
7. After the flight, the motor casing is hot. A grown-up takes it out once it cools.

## Activity 1 ★ Launch!

**Grown-up steps for each flight (printer track).** Follow the motor maker's instructions and your build notes; this is the order:

1. Check the wind, the field and the area around the pad (no dry grass).
2. Range safety check: rocket in good shape, nose slides off, shock cord tied, swing-tested on Day 9.
3. Put flame-resistant wadding into the body, then the packed parachute and the shock cord, then the nose. (Kids can help here.)
4. **Grown-up:** put the igniter into the motor's nozzle and hold it in with its plug. Put the motor in the rocket and lock the motor retainer.
5. **Grown-up:** slide the rocket onto the launch rod. With the key **out**, clip the wires to the igniter, keeping the clips from touching each other.
6. Everyone goes back to the controller, at least 15 feet away. Trackers go to their baselines.
7. **Grown-up:** puts in the key and checks the light.
8. **Kid countdown caller:** "Range is clear! 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" The grown-up presses and holds the button until it lifts off.
9. **Grown-up:** takes out the key right away.
10. Trackers pinch their inclinometer strings at apogee. Timers time **liftoff to parachute** and **parachute to landing**.
11. The recovery crew goes when the grown-up says so, and notes how far it landed from the pad (count paces).

**Paper track launch:** fly stomp rockets (track their height with two inclinometers), a fizzy rocket contest (which water amount wins?), and parachute drops. Or visit a local NAR club launch: most welcome visitors, and you can watch dozens of real rockets fly.

## Activity 2 ★ Flight log and comparison

1. Fill in the **Flight log** for every flight.
2. Work out each altitude: baseline × multiplier (or × tan of the angle) + eye height. Average your trackers.
3. Compare with the prediction: Rocket Builder's predicted altitude (printer track), or your guess before the flight (paper track).
4. Write why they might be different. Ideas: wind tilted the path; the real rocket is heavier or lighter than the app thought; rough paint or glue makes more drag; tracking errors; real motors vary a little from one to the next.

**Example:** predicted 140 m. Trackers read 50° and 54° from a 100 m baseline. Multipliers 1.19 and 1.38, so 119 m and 138 m (plus 1.5 m eye height). Average ≈ 130 m. That's about 10 m lower than predicted.

## Capstone project: mission report

Each student makes a mission report: a poster, a short slide show, a written report, or a 3–5 minute talk. It must have these parts (the **Capstone planner** in `worksheets.html` lists them):

1. **Mission:** what you set out to do.
2. **Design:** a picture of your rocket (a screenshot or a drawing), with the balance point and push point marked, and its stability score. Why did you choose this design? Use data from your Day 6 log.
3. **The science:** explain in your own words (a) how Newton's third law makes a rocket go, (b) why the balance point must be in front of the push point, and (c) how a parachute works.
4. **Prediction vs flight:** your flight log numbers next to the prediction, and a graph or table.
5. **What happened and why:** explain any differences.
6. **Next time:** one change you'd make, and what you predict it would do.
7. **Safety:** two rules your team followed, and why they matter.

### Rubric

Score each row from 1 to 4. **Total: 20 points.** 17–20 = mission accomplished; 13–16 = solid flight; 9–12 = needs another iteration; below 9 = back to the pad.

| | 4: Excellent | 3: Good | 2: Getting there | 1: Just starting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Science** | Explains Newton's 3rd law, stability (CG ahead of CP) and parachutes correctly, in own words, with examples | Explains all three, with small mistakes | Explains one or two | Ideas are missing or mostly incorrect |
| **Design process** | Shows several iterations with data, changes one thing at a time, and uses the data to justify the choice | Shows iterations and a reason | Shows a design with little data | Design with no reasons |
| **Data and math** | Flight log complete; altitude calculated correctly; prediction compared (grade 8: percent difference) | Log mostly complete; small calculation errors | Some data; calculation incomplete | Little or no data |
| **Explaining results** | Gives two or more sensible reasons for differences, and a testable next step | One reason and a next step | A reason *or* a next step | Neither |
| **Communication and safety** | Clear, organised, labelled pictures and graphs; names two safety rules and why | Clear, with one missing piece | Hard to follow in places | Missing most parts |

## Discussion questions

1. Which part of the flight matched the prediction best? Which matched worst?
2. Where did you see each of Newton's laws today?
3. Did the parachute come out near apogee? Was the delay about right?
4. How far did the rocket drift? Did the wind direction match where it landed?
5. If you flew again tomorrow, what one thing would you change?

## Quick check

1. In which order do these happen: apogee, liftoff, burnout, landing, parachute opens?
2. What makes the rocket go up, in terms of Newton's third law?
3. Your trackers measured 120 m and 130 m. What's the average?
4. A rocket was predicted to fly 150 m and flew 120 m. How many metres short was it? (Grade 8: what percent?)
5. Who takes the spent motor out of the rocket?

**Answer key**

1. Liftoff, burnout, apogee, parachute opens, landing.
2. The motor pushes hot gas down, and the gas pushes the rocket up.
3. (120 + 130) ÷ 2 = **125 m**.
4. 150 − 120 = **30 m** short. Percent difference = 30 ÷ 150 × 100 = **20%**.
5. A grown-up, after it cools.

## Differentiation

**Grade 5:** be the countdown caller or a timer, and do one tracking angle with the multiplier table. The mission report can be a labelled poster and a short talk.

**Grade 8 extension:**

- Use tan and two trackers. Work out the percent difference from the prediction for each flight.
- Average speed on the way up: altitude ÷ time from liftoff to apogee. Why is it much lower than Rocket Builder's top speed?
- Descent rate from your timing: altitude ÷ time under the parachute. Compare with the app's descent speed.
- Make a graph comparing predicted and measured altitudes for every flight (yours, or several from a club launch).

## Track notes

**Printer track:** first flight on the smallest motor that gets a ✓ in Rocket Builder. If the rocket comes back in good shape, you can fly it again with a new motor and fresh wadding. Log every flight.

**Paper track:** if you'd like to fly a real rocket later, Rocket Builder can order a printed version of your design, and a local NAR club can help with your first launch.
