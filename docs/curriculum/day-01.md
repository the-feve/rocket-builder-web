# Day 1: What is a rocket? Newton's three laws

[← Overview](README.md) · [Day 2 →](day-02.md)

**Time:** about 75 minutes. **Worksheet:** W1 Balloon rocket data (in `worksheets.html`).

## Objectives

By the end of today your student can:

- Say what makes a rocket different from a plane or a car (it carries everything it pushes out).
- Explain Newton's three laws in their own words, with an example of each.
- Use the third law to explain how a balloon rocket and a real rocket move.
- Run a fair test, record three trials and find the average.

## Vocabulary

- **Force**: a push or a pull. We measure force in **newtons (N)**. 1 N is about the weight of a 100 g apple.
- **Mass**: how much stuff is in something. Measured in grams (g) or kilograms (kg).
- **Inertia**: things keep doing what they're doing (staying still or moving) unless a force changes it.
- **Acceleration**: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
- **Action and reaction**: when one thing pushes another, the second thing pushes back just as hard, the opposite way.
- **Thrust**: the push that moves a rocket forward.
- **Rocket**: a vehicle that moves by pushing stuff (usually hot gas) out the back, and carries that stuff with it.

## Materials

Household:

- Long balloons (round ones work too), 3 or more
- String or fishing line, 5 m or longer, and two chairs or door handles to tie it to
- A drinking straw, tape, scissors
- A tape measure, a stopwatch or phone
- 10–20 paper clips
- A piece of string to measure balloon size (or a soft tape measure)

To buy: nothing. (12+ track: a grown-up starts ordering supplies today; see the track notes below.)

## Teach (read aloud or adapt)

> What do a firework, a space rocket and a balloon you let go of have in common? They all push something out the back, and they all shoot forward.
>
> About 340 years ago, a scientist named Isaac Newton wrote down three rules that explain how things move. We still use them to send rockets to space.
>
> **Law 1: Things keep doing what they're doing.** A ball sitting on the floor stays still until something pushes it. A rocket on the pad stays put until its motor pushes harder than gravity pulls. A hockey puck sliding on ice keeps sliding for a long time, because almost nothing is slowing it down. This "keep doing it" is called *inertia*.
>
> **Law 2: A bigger push gives a bigger change, and more mass gives a smaller change.** Push an empty shopping cart and it zooms. Push a full one just as hard and it barely speeds up. Scientists write it as **force = mass × acceleration**, or **F = m × a**. That's why rocket builders work so hard to keep rockets light.
>
> **Law 3: Every push has an equal push back.** When you jump off a skateboard, you push the board backward and the board pushes you forward. A rocket motor pushes hot gas out the back very hard. The gas pushes the rocket forward just as hard. That's thrust!
>
> Here's a surprise: a rocket does **not** push against the air or the ground. It pushes on its own exhaust. That's why rockets work in space, where there's no air at all.
>
> A rocket is special because it carries everything it pushes out. A plane pushes on the air around it. A rocket brings its own "push stuff" along.

**Quick demos while you talk (optional):**

- *Law 1:* put a playing card on top of a cup and a coin on the card. Flick the card away fast. The coin stays put and drops into the cup.
- *Law 2:* roll a toy car with one gentle push. Tape some coins on it and give the same push. It goes slower.
- *Law 3:* two people on rolling chairs push palms. Both roll apart, not just the one who pushed.

## Activity 1 ★ Balloon rocket on a string

You'll test two things: **how much air** (more push) and **how much mass** (more to move).

**Set up**

1. Thread the string through the straw.
2. Tie one end of the string to a chair or door handle. Pull the string tight and tie the other end at the same height, at least 4 m away.
3. Mark a start line on the string with a bit of tape.

**Part A: more air**

1. Blow up a balloon a little (small). Pinch the end shut. Don't tie it.
2. Wrap the measuring string around the fattest part and measure it (that's the *circumference*). Write it down.
3. Tape the balloon to the straw, with the opening pointing back toward the start.
4. Slide the straw to the start line. Let go!
5. Measure how far the straw went from the start line. That's one trial.
6. Do it 3 times with the same size balloon. Try to blow it to the same circumference each time.
7. Repeat with a medium and a large balloon.
8. Find the average distance for each size (add the 3 trials, divide by 3).

**Part B: more mass**

1. Use a medium balloon each time (same circumference).
2. Do 3 trials with 0 paper clips, then 3 with 5 paper clips taped to the straw, then 3 with 10.
3. Measure and average, like before.

**Tips:** if the balloon reaches the end of the string every time, time how long it takes instead, or make the string longer. Keep the string tight and level.

**What to expect:** bigger balloons push for longer and go further. More paper clips mean more mass, so the same push gives less speed, and the rocket doesn't go as far.

## Worksheet

**W1 Balloon rocket data**: tables for Part A and Part B, averages, and three Newton's-law questions.

## Discussion questions

1. Where in the balloon rocket do you see each of Newton's three laws?
2. Why does a real rocket have to be as light as possible?
3. A rocket in space has no air to push against. How can it still speed up?
4. When you blow up the balloon more, what changes: the push, how long it pushes, or both?
5. What made a trial "unfair"? How could we make our tests fairer?

## Quick check

1. A rocket pushes gas down. The gas pushes the rocket up. Which of Newton's laws is this?
2. Two balloon rockets get the same push. One has 10 paper clips. Which speeds up more?
3. A rocket sits on the launch pad. Why doesn't it move until the motor fires?
4. True or false: a rocket needs air to push against.
5. Write F = m × a in words.

**Answer key**

1. The third law (action and reaction).
2. The one with no paper clips (less mass, so more acceleration from the same force: law 2).
3. The forces on it are balanced (the pad holds it up against gravity). It stays still until a bigger force (thrust) acts on it: law 1.
4. False. It pushes on its own exhaust gas, so it works in space too.
5. Force equals mass times acceleration. (Or: a bigger push gives a bigger speed-up; more mass gives a smaller speed-up.)

## Differentiation

**Younger kids (grades 3–6):** focus on law 3 ("push back"). Do Part A with 2 balloon sizes. Grades 3–4: skip the averages and circle the longest of the three trials. Grades 5–6: find the averages with a calculator.

**Grade 7–8 extension:**

- Find the average speed: time the straw over a measured distance, then speed = distance ÷ time.
- Use F = m × a. A balloon pushes with about 0.1 N, and the straw, tape and balloon have a mass of 10 g (0.010 kg). Ignoring friction, what's the acceleration? (a = F ÷ m = 0.1 ÷ 0.010 = **10 m/s²**.) Add 10 g of paper clips: a = 0.1 ÷ 0.020 = **5 m/s²**. Doubling the mass halves the acceleration.
- Make a graph: number of paper clips (x) vs average distance (y). Is it a straight line?
