# Rockets and Model Rocketry: a 2-week home-school unit

_For about grades 5–8. Goes with **Rocket Builder by Quiver Design Works** (https://rocket.quiverdesignworks.net), a free web app for designing 3D-printable model rockets._

This unit teaches the science behind model rockets: forces, motion, stability, drag, motors, parachutes and measuring height. Each day has a short lesson a parent can read aloud, one or two hands-on activities, a worksheet, discussion questions and a quick check. It ends with a launch day and a small project.

You don't need a 3D printer. There are two tracks:

- **Printer track.** Your student designs a rocket in Rocket Builder, you print it, and an adult launches it with a real model rocket motor on Day 10. (No printer? Rocket Builder can also *order a printed rocket* for you, and a local rocket club can help you fly it.)
- **Paper track.** Everything is done with straw rockets, balloon rockets, a stomp rocket and fizzy film-canister rockets. Rocket Builder is still used as a free "rocket lab" on screen, because it's a great way to test ideas without building anything.

> **Adults handle all rocket motors and igniters.** Kids design, build, check, count down and track. A grown-up buys, prepares, loads and launches the motors, every time, following the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code (see [Safety](#safety)).

## How each day works

Each day takes about **60–90 minutes**, plus optional extras. Every lesson has the same parts:

1. **Objectives**: what your student will be able to do.
2. **Vocabulary**: new words (they are also on the worksheet).
3. **Materials**: household things first, then anything to buy.
4. **Teach**: a short talk to read aloud or put in your own words (5–10 minutes).
5. **Activities**: one or two hands-on activities, step by step.
6. **Worksheet**: a page in `worksheets.html`.
7. **Discussion questions**.
8. **Quick check**: 3–5 short questions, with answers.
9. **Differentiation**: easier for grade 5, harder (with more math) for grade 8.
10. **Track notes**: what's different with or without a 3D printer.

### Short on time? A 1-week version

Do two days' worth each day. Keep the activity marked ★ in each lesson and skip or shorten the rest.

| Week day | Combine | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Days 1 + 2 | Balloon rocket ★, straw rockets ★ (skip the launch-angle test) |
| 2 | Days 3 + 4 | Swing test ★, nose-cone test ★ |
| 3 | Days 5 + 6 | Motor codes worksheet, Rocket Builder design challenge ★ (fizzy rockets become optional) |
| 4 | Days 7 + 8 | Parachute drop test ★, inclinometer ★ (measure one tall object) |
| 5 | Days 9 + 10 | Safety code and preflight ★, launch day ★, a short spoken mission report |

**Printer track in 1 week:** a print takes several hours, and a tall rocket is split into sections. Design the rocket on Monday evening (use Rocket Builder's Beginner mode and the defaults if you're short on time), print Tuesday to Thursday, and build on Thursday. Or plan the real launch for the weekend after, or at a local club launch.

### Plan ahead (printer track)

- **Day 1:** buy or order motors, a launch pad and controller, recovery wadding and a parachute (see the list below). Many hobby and craft stores sell Estes motors; buying in a store avoids shipping fees for motors.
- **By Day 5:** find a launch field big enough for your motor (Day 5 and Day 9 explain field size). A local **NAR club launch** is a great option: they have the pads, a big field and experienced fliers. Find one at nar.org.
- **Day 6:** start printing.
- **Day 8:** assemble.
- **Day 10:** pick a backup day in case of wind or rain.

### Household (both tracks)

- Printer paper, cardstock or old cereal boxes, scrap cardboard
- Clear tape, masking tape, glue stick, scissors, ruler, pencils
- Drinking straws (two widths if you have them), a few balloons (long ones work best)
- String or fishing line (5–10 m), paper clips, binder clips

## Safety

Read this page before Day 1, and again before Day 10.

### The one big rule

**Adults handle all rocket motors and igniters.** That means buying, storing, preparing, putting the igniter in, putting the rocket on the pad, connecting the clips, holding the safety key and pressing the launch button. Kids can do the countdown, and they can design, build, check and track. Kids never carry the launch key.

### NAR Model Rocket Safety Code (the main points)

This is a short, plain summary of the National Association of Rocketry's code. Read the official code at nar.org before launch day.

1. **Materials.** Rockets are made of light materials such as paper, wood, rubber and plastic. No metal for the nose, body or fins. (Printed PLA plastic is fine.)
2. **Motors.** Use only certified, store-bought model rocket motors, exactly as the maker says. Never change a motor or reload it. Store motors away from heat and flames.
3. **Ignition.** Launch with an electrical launch system and electric igniters. The controller has a **safety key**, and its button springs back to "off" when you let go.
4. **Misfires.** If the rocket doesn't launch when the button is pressed, take out the safety key (or disconnect the battery) and **wait 60 seconds** before anyone walks up to it.
5. **Launch safety.** Count down out loud from 5 so everyone knows. Everyone pays attention and stands **at least 15 feet (about 5 m) away** for D motors or smaller, and 30 feet for bigger ones. Check the rocket is stable before its first flight.
6. **Launcher.** Use a launch rod or rail pointed within 30 degrees of straight up, with a blast deflector. Clear dry grass and anything that burns from around the pad. Keep the top of the rod above eye level or cover it when not in use.
7. **Size.** A model rocket weighs no more than 1,500 g at liftoff. (All the rockets in this unit are far smaller.)
8. **Flight safety.** Never launch at targets, into clouds, near airplanes or over people.
9. **Launch site.** Outdoors, in a large open area away from tall trees, power lines and buildings. **Not in winds over 20 mph.** The field must be at least as big as the table below.
10. **Recovery.** Use a parachute or streamer so the rocket comes down gently and can fly again. Use only flame-resistant wadding.
11. **Recovery safety.** Never try to get a rocket out of a power line, a tall tree or another dangerous place. Never try to catch a rocket as it comes down.

**Minimum field size (from the NAR code; also used by Rocket Builder):**

| Motor class | Total impulse (N·s) | Smallest field side |
|---|---|---|
| 1/4A, 1/2A | up to 1.25 | 50 ft (15 m) |
| A | 1.26–2.5 | 100 ft (30 m) |
| B | 2.51–5 | 200 ft (60 m) |
| C | 5.01–10 | 400 ft (120 m) |
| D | 10.01–20 | 500 ft (150 m) |
| E, F | 20.01–80 | 1,000 ft (300 m) |

This is the **smallest** field. A rocket also drifts sideways in the wind under its parachute, so Rocket Builder often suggests a longer field. Day 9 shows how to work that out.

Also check: local rules (some towns, parks and states have their own), fire danger (don't launch when the grass is dry and there's a burn ban), and stay well away from airports.

### Safety for this week's activities

- **Eye protection** for straw rockets, stomp rockets and fizzy film-canister rockets, for everyone nearby.
- **Never aim** any rocket, straw or stomp rocket at a person, animal or window.
- **Fizzy tablets are medicine.** Original Alka-Seltzer contains aspirin. An adult keeps the box and hands out the pieces. Plain fizzy vitamin C tablets work too. Do fizzy rockets outside, and never lean over a canister. If one doesn't pop, wait at least 30 seconds before picking it up, and point it away from faces.
- **Swing test:** outdoors, nobody within 3 m, short string (about 1 m).
- **Inclinometer:** never look at the sun through the straw.
- **3D printer:** the nozzle and bed are hot. A grown-up runs the printer, in a room with fresh air.
- **Launch day:** follow the [launch day rules](day-10.md#launch-day-rules).

## A note on the numbers

The numbers here match Rocket Builder: stability in calibers (1–2 cal is ideal, under 1 is too wobbly to download, over 3 is too stable); thrust at least **5 times** the weight; at least **15 m/s** off the launch rod; at least **30 m** high so the parachute can open; and a parachute that brings the rocket down at about **3–5 m/s**. The app's numbers are quick estimates. Real flights differ a bit, which is part of the fun on Day 10: compare the prediction with what really happened.
