# Lesson 4: Drag and shape

**Time:** about 55 minutes. **Worksheet:** W4 Drag test. **Rocket Builder:** Beginner mode; Advanced for the extension.

## Standards

| Code | In plain words |
|---|---|
| **MS-ETS1-2** | Evaluate competing designs with a systematic process. |
| **MS-ETS1-3** | Analyze test data to find the best features of several designs. |
| **6.SP.B.4** (math) | Display data (bar graph or dot plot). |

**Practice:** analyzing and interpreting data. **Crosscutting concept:** structure and function.

## I can…

- I can list what makes drag bigger: speed, frontal area, shape and roughness.
- I can run a fair test of nose shapes, changing one variable.
- I can explain the fin trade-off: stability versus drag and weight.

## Words to know

- **Drag**: air resisting motion. Double the speed and drag goes up about 4 times.
- **Frontal area**: the size of the front, seen from the nose.
- **Drag coefficient (Cd)**: a number for how slippery a shape is; model rockets are about 0.5–0.75.
- **Trade-off**: improving one thing makes another worse.

## Materials

Straw-rocket supplies, a squeeze-bottle launcher (bottle + straw sealed into the cap with clay), a tape measure, a kitchen scale (optional), **safety glasses**, W4.

## Steps

### 1. Teach (10 minutes)

> Four things make drag bigger: speed, a bigger front, a blunt shape, and roughness. Fins keep a rocket stable, but every fin adds drag and weight, so designers want fins that are just big enough. Today: change **one thing at a time**.

### 2. Nose-cone test (20 minutes)

<!-- figure: squeeze-launcher -->

1. Three identical straw rockets: **flat**, **cone** and **round** noses, same mass (add tape to the lighter ones).
2. Same launcher, same angle. 3 trials each. Mean and bar graph on W4.

### 3. Fin test (15 minutes)

No fins, 3 small, 3 medium, 4 large. 3 trials each. Record distance and "straight or wobbly".

### 4. Rocket Builder (5 minutes)

Keep everything the same and change only the nose shape; build each. The score barely changes: at model-rocket speeds the fins matter far more than the nose.

### 5. Wrap up (5 minutes)

Which design had the best features? Could you combine them?

## Check for understanding

1. Name three things that make drag bigger.
2. A rocket goes twice as fast. How much bigger is the drag?
3. Why must the three rockets weigh the same?
4. Why not put huge fins on every rocket?

**Answer key**

1. Speed, frontal area, blunt shape, roughness (any three).
2. About 4 times.
3. So only the nose shape changes (a fair test).
4. Extra drag and weight lower the flight, and it can be over-stable and turn into the wind.

## Teacher notes

- **What to expect:** flat noses go shortest; cone and round are often close, so small differences may be luck. More trials or a fairer launcher (a book dropped on the bottle from a set height) help.
- **Extension (Advanced mode):** drag = ½ × 1.2 × speed² × Cd × area. A 30 mm rocket (area ≈ 0.00071 m²), Cd 0.65 at 50 m/s: ≈ 0.7 N, about its own weight. In **Advanced → Flight**, set the drag coefficient to 0.5 and then 0.8, build each time and compare the predicted altitude.
- **Further resources:** Apogee Components' free lesson *Rocket Optimization* (apogeerockets.com/downloads/PDFs/58-2.pdf).
- **Change one thing:** the launch simulator's change-one-thing experiments fly the same rocket with one setting changed (drag coefficient, nose, fins) and compare the flights.
