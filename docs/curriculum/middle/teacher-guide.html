Teacher guide · Grades 6–8

How to use this guide

Nine lessons, about 55–60 minutes each, plus a launch day. Built on the Washington State K–12 Science Learning Standards (the NGSS). Goes with Rocket Builder by Quiver Design Works (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net), in Beginner mode first and Advanced mode from Lesson 6.

Students investigate forces, stability, drag, motors and energy with paper rockets, then design a rocket in Rocket Builder, 3D-print it (or order it printed) and fly it on a real model rocket motor. Adults handle all motors and igniters, every time, under the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code. Motor flights and kits are rated 12+: students under 12 (some 6th graders) do every lesson and take launch-day jobs (tracker, recorder, countdown caller), and keep their designs on screen.

A note on the numbers

They match Rocket Builder: stability 1–2 cal is ideal, under 1 is too wobbly to download, over 3 is over-stable; thrust at least 5 times the weight; at least 15 m/s off the rod; at least 30 m high so the parachute can open; a parachute that brings the rocket down at about 3–5 m/s. The app's numbers are estimates. Comparing them with the real flight is the point of Lesson 9.

Teacher guide · Grades 6–8

Safety

Read this page before Lesson 1, and again before launch day (Lesson 9).

The big rules

  1. Motors are 12+, and only adults handle them. Model rocket motors and Rocket Builder kits are rated 12+. An adult buys, stores and prepares every motor, puts in the igniter, puts the rocket on the pad, connects the clips, holds the safety key and presses the launch button. Students design, build, check, count down, track and record. Students never carry the launch key. Students under 12 (some 6th graders) do every lesson but take launch-day jobs away from the pad.
  2. Glasses on for straw, stomp and fizzy rockets and at the pad, for everyone nearby.
  3. Never aim any rocket at a person, animal or window.

Classroom activities

  • Straw and stomp rockets: glasses on; aim up or along an empty space; wait until everyone is behind the launcher.
  • Fizzy film-canister demo (optional): adult-led only. The adult keeps the tablets (Alka-Seltzer contains aspirin). Outside, glasses on, never lean over a canister. If one doesn't pop, wait at least 30 seconds; the adult picks it up pointing away from faces.
  • Swing test: outdoors, nobody within 3 m, a string about 1 m long. It's a demonstration only.
  • Parachute drops from a height: an adult does the dropping.
  • Inclinometer: never look at the sun through the straw.
  • 3D printer: the nozzle and bed are hot. An adult runs the printer, in a room with fresh air. Adults handle the glue.

The NAR Model Rocket Safety Code (a plain summary)

The National Association of Rocketry's code is what every launch in this unit follows. Read the official code at nar.org before launch day.

  1. Materials. Light materials such as paper, wood, rubber and plastic. No metal for the nose, body or fins. (Printed PLA is fine.)
  2. Motors. Only certified, store-bought model rocket motors, used exactly as the maker says. Never change, open or reload one. Store them away from heat and flames.
  3. Ignition. An electrical launch system with electric igniters. The controller has a safety key, and its button springs back to "off".
  4. Misfires. If it doesn't launch, take out the safety key (or disconnect the battery) and wait 60 seconds before anyone walks up.
  5. Launch safety. Count down out loud from 5. Everyone pays attention and stands at least 15 feet (about 5 m) away for D motors or smaller, 30 feet for bigger ones. Check the rocket is stable before its first flight.
  6. Launcher. A rod or rail within 30 degrees of straight up, with a blast deflector. Clear dry grass and anything that burns from around the pad. Keep the rod top above eye level or covered.
  7. Size. No more than 1,500 g at liftoff. (All the rockets in this unit are far smaller.)
  8. Flight safety. Never launch at targets, into clouds, near airplanes or over people.
  9. Launch site. Outdoors, in a large open area away from tall trees, power lines and buildings. Not in winds over 20 mph. The field must be at least as big as the table below.
  10. Recovery. A parachute or streamer, with flame-resistant wadding.
  11. Recovery safety. Never try to get a rocket out of a power line, a tall tree or another dangerous place. Never try to catch a rocket.

Minimum field size (from the NAR code; also used by Rocket Builder):

Motor class Total impulse (N·s) Smallest field side
1/4A, 1/2A up to 1.25 50 ft (15 m)
A 1.26–2.5 100 ft (30 m)
B 2.51–5 200 ft (60 m)
C 5.01–10 400 ft (120 m)
D 10.01–20 500 ft (150 m)
E, F 20.01–80 1,000 ft (300 m)

This is the smallest field. A rocket drifts under its parachute, so Rocket Builder often suggests a longer one (Lesson 7 shows how to work it out).

Also check local rules (towns, parks and school districts may have their own), fire danger and burn bans, and stay well away from airports.

Launch day rules

  1. Adults only: motors, igniters, the launch key, loading the pad and clipping the wires.
  2. Safety glasses at the pad; everyone at least 15 feet back when a rocket is armed (30 feet for motors bigger than D); a class stays farther back behind a marked spectator line.
  3. Wind under 20 mph. No launching near trees, power lines, buildings, roads or people who aren't part of the launch.
  4. Count down out loud from 5. Everyone watches the rocket until it's on the ground.
  5. Misfire: the adult takes out the key and everyone waits 60 seconds before going to the pad.
  6. The recovery crew goes only when the adult in charge says so. Never catch a rocket.
  7. After the flight the motor casing is hot. An adult takes it out once it cools.
Lesson 1 of 9 · Grades 6–8

Newton's laws and the balloon rocket

Lesson 1

Time: about 55 minutes. Worksheet: W1 Balloon rocket data. Rocket Builder: Beginner mode, first look.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-PS2-2 Plan an investigation showing that a change in motion depends on the total force and the mass.
MS-PS2-1 Use Newton's third law (equal and opposite forces) to explain and design.
6.SP.B.5 (math) Summarize data: mean of repeated trials.

Practice: planning and carrying out investigations. Crosscutting concept: cause and effect.

I can…

  • I can state Newton's three laws in my own words, with an example of each.
  • I can use the third law to explain how a balloon and a real rocket move.
  • I can run a fair test that changes one variable (mass) and find the mean of three trials.

Words to know

  • Force: a push or pull, measured in newtons (N). 1 N is about the weight of a 100 g apple.
  • Mass: how much matter something has (g, kg).
  • Inertia: objects keep doing what they're doing unless a net force acts.
  • Acceleration: a change in speed or direction.
  • Thrust: the force that pushes a rocket forward.

Materials

Per group: 2–3 long balloons, 5 m of string tied between two chairs, a straw, tape, 10 paper clips, a binder clip, a tape measure, a string to measure the balloon's circumference, worksheet W1.

Steps

1 Hook (5 minutes)

Let go of an untied balloon. What do this, a firework and a space rocket have in common? (They push something out the back and move the other way.)

2 Teach (10 minutes)

Read aloud

Law 1: objects keep doing what they're doing (still, or moving in a straight line) unless a net force changes it. A rocket sits on the pad until the motor pushes harder than gravity pulls.

Law 2: force = mass × acceleration (F = m × a). The same push gives a light object a big speed-up and a heavy one a small speed-up. That's why rockets are built light.

Law 3: forces come in pairs, equal and opposite. A motor pushes gas out the back; the gas pushes the rocket forward. A rocket doesn't push on the air or the ground, so it works in space.

3 Investigate (30 minutes)

  1. Thread the straw on the string; tie the string level and tight, at least 4 m long.
  2. Blow a balloon to a measured circumference (e.g. 50 cm). Hold it shut with the binder clip; tape it to the straw.
  3. Release from the start line and measure the distance. 3 trials, same circumference each time.
  4. Tape 5 paper clips to the straw: 3 trials. Then 10: 3 trials.
  5. Find the mean for each mass on W1.

Variable changed: mass. Kept the same: balloon size (push), string, start line.

4 Rocket Builder (5 minutes)

Open Rocket Builder (Beginner). Follow the tutorial to Build my rocket, then press ▶ Watch it launch. Note three things you see.

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

Where is each law in the balloon rocket?

Check for understanding

  1. A rocket pushes gas down; the gas pushes the rocket up. Which law?
  2. Two balloon rockets get the same push. One carries 10 paper clips. Which accelerates more, and why?
  3. True or false: a rocket needs air to push against.
  4. Your trials with 5 clips were 3.2 m, 3.6 m and 3.4 m. What's the mean?
Answer key
  1. The third law.
  2. The one with no clips: the same force on less mass gives more acceleration (second law).
  3. False: it pushes on its own exhaust.
  4. (3.2 + 3.6 + 3.4) ÷ 3 = 3.4 m.

Teacher notes

  • What to expect: more paper clips, shorter distance. If balloons always reach the end, lengthen the string or time the run instead.
  • About MS-PS2-1: the PE is written about collisions. Here students apply the third law to a push-apart interaction (rocket and exhaust); treat it as a partial match and pick up collisions elsewhere if you need the full PE.
  • Extension (F = m × a): a balloon pushes about 0.1 N on a 10 g (0.010 kg) rocket: a = 0.1 ÷ 0.010 = 10 m/s². Add 10 g: 5 m/s². Doubling the mass halves the acceleration. Graph clips vs mean distance.
  • Students under 12: some 6th graders are 11. They do every lesson; the printed rocket's motor flight (Lesson 9) is for 12+, and adults handle all motors. Under-12 students design on screen, track and record.
  • Plan ahead (adult): order the launch pad and controller, wadding and motors now, and look for a field or a local NAR club launch (nar.org). Wait for Lesson 6 to pick the motor class; the common 18 mm (M18) size is a safe bet.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free lesson Rockets and the Third Law (apogeerockets.com/downloads/PDFs/58-1.pdf, grades 5–8).
Lesson 2 of 9 · Grades 6–8

The forces in a rocket flight

Lesson 2

Time: about 55 minutes. Worksheet: W2 Forces and launch angles. Rocket Builder: Beginner mode.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-PS2-2 A change in motion depends on the sum of the forces and the mass.
MS-PS2-4 (connection) Gravity pulls masses together; a rocket's weight is Earth's pull on its mass.
6.RP.A.3 (math) Ratio reasoning: thrust-to-weight.

Practice: developing and using models (force diagrams). Crosscutting concept: systems and system models.

I can…

  • I can draw force diagrams for each part of a flight: on the pad, boost, coast, parachute.
  • I can tell balanced from unbalanced forces and say what each does to motion.
  • I can check a thrust-to-weight ratio of at least 5 to 1.

Words to know

  • Thrust, weight, drag, lift: the four forces. Weight always points down; drag points opposite to the motion; fins make lift that steers the nose back into line.
  • Net force: what's left when you add all the forces.
  • Apogee: the highest point of the flight.
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio: thrust ÷ weight.

Materials

Paper strips (8 × 20 cm), pencils, straws, cardstock, tape, scissors, a protractor taped to a box, a tape measure, safety glasses, W2.

Steps

1 Teach (10 minutes)

Read aloud

On the pad, the weight pulls down and the pad pushes up: balanced, so nothing changes. At liftoff the thrust is far bigger than weight plus drag, so the rocket accelerates. Model rockets need thrust at least 5 times their weight, so they leave the rod fast enough for the fins to steer. After burnout there's no thrust; weight and drag both slow it as it coasts to apogee. Under the parachute, drag up equals weight down: balanced again, so it falls at a steady speed.

2 Model it (10 minutes)

On W2, draw and label the forces in the four flight pictures. Longer arrow = bigger force. Mark which pictures are balanced.

3 Investigate: straw rockets and launch angle (25 minutes)

  1. Roll a paper tube around a pencil, tape it, close the nose airtight, add 3 small fins.
  2. Glasses on. Launch at 15°, 30°, 45°, 60° and 75°, 2–3 trials each, the same breath each time (or a squeeze bottle).
  3. Record and graph mean distance against angle.

4 Rocket Builder (5 minutes)

▶ Watch it launch: name each phase aloud. In the report, read the power bar (thrust ÷ weight). Is it 5× or more?

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

Why does the rocket keep going up after the motor stops? (Inertia: it's moving up; weight and drag slow it, they don't stop it at once.)

Check for understanding

  1. During the coast, which forces act on the rocket?
  2. Thrust 6 N, weight 1 N, drag 0.5 N. What's the net force, and which way?
  3. Is thrust 6 N on a 1.2 N rocket at least 5 times the weight?
  4. Under a parachute at steady speed, are the forces balanced?
Answer key
  1. Weight and drag (no thrust).
  2. 6 − 1 − 0.5 = 4.5 N up.
  3. 6 ÷ 1.2 = 5: just passes.
  4. Yes: drag up equals weight down.

Teacher notes

  • What to expect: about 40–45° usually goes farthest. Real model rockets launch nearly straight up so they land inside the field.
  • Extension: net force 4.5 N on a 0.1 kg rocket: a = 45 m/s², about 4½ times gravity. Draw arrows to scale (1 cm = 1 N).
  • About MS-PS2-4: this lesson only connects to it (weight = gravity on mass); the full PE needs an argument about masses and gravitational attraction.
  • Safety: glasses on for everyone; never aim at people; fetch only on "clear".
  • Force arrows and graphs: in the launch simulator, turn on the force arrows and open the flight graphs (altitude, speed and acceleration against time). Ask students to find boost, coast and apogee on the speed graph.
Lesson 3 of 9 · Grades 6–8

Stability: the balance point and the push point

Lesson 3

Time: about 60 minutes. Worksheet: W3 Swing test. Rocket Builder: Beginner mode.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-ETS1-3 Analyze test data from several designs to find the best features of each.
MS-PS2-2 Forces (here, the air on the fins) change an object's motion.
6.EE.A.2 (math) Evaluate a formula: score = (CP − CG) ÷ body width.

Practice: analyzing and interpreting data. Crosscutting concept: stability and change.

I can…

  • I can find a rocket's balance point (CG) and estimate its push point (CP).
  • I can explain why the CG must be in front of the CP.
  • I can work out a stability score in calibers and say whether it's safe (1–2 cal is ideal).

Words to know

  • Centre of gravity (CG), balance point: Rocket Builder's blue dot.
  • Centre of pressure (CP), push point: where the air's push acts on average. The red dot.
  • Caliber (cal): one body width, used as a ruler.
  • Stability margin (score): how far the CP is behind the CG, in calibers.

Materials

Paper-towel tube, cardstock nose and fins, modeling clay, 1.5 m of string, scrap cardboard, a ruler, W3.

Steps

1 Teach (10 minutes)

Read aloud

Fins are a rocket's feathers. If a rocket tilts, air hits its side. When the push point is behind the balance point, the air swings the tail back in line, like a weathervane. If the push point is in front, the air swings the nose the wrong way and the rocket tumbles.

Rocket Builder's score: under 1 cal too wobbly (no download), 1–2 ideal, 2–3 fine, over 3 so stable it turns into the wind. The app checks it with the heaviest motor loaded, the worst case. To fix a low score: bigger or swept fins, a longer body, or nose weight.

2 Measure (15 minutes)

  1. Build a test rocket (tube, cone nose, 3–4 fins). Balance it on a finger: mark the CG (blue). Measure from the nose tip.
  2. Trace the rocket's side view on cardboard, cut it out and balance the cut-out: that's a cautious estimate of the CP (red). Mark it.
  3. Score = (CP − CG) ÷ body width.

3 Swing-test demo (20 minutes)

Outdoors, nobody within 3 m. Tie the string at the CG; swing the rocket in a circle; does the nose lead? Test A no fins, B fins, C fins + nose clay, D fins + tail clay. Re-find the CG each time. Record on W3.

4 Rocket Builder (10 minutes)

Build a rocket. Find the blue and red dots and the score. Change only the fin shape and rebuild. Which fins give the highest score?

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

Which version was best, and what did the data show about why?

Check for understanding

  1. Which must be in front: CG or CP?
  2. Body 2 cm wide; CP is 3 cm behind the CG. Score?
  3. Is 0.5 cal OK to fly?
  4. Name two ways to raise the score.
Answer key
  1. The CG (blue) in front of the CP (red).
  2. 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 cal.
  3. No: wobbly; Rocket Builder won't let you download it.
  4. Bigger or swept-back fins, a longer body, weight in the nose.

Teacher notes

  • The swing test is a demonstration, not proof. It can give wrong answers (string position, swing speed). For the real rocket, the go/no-go check is the balance point: with a motor in, the measured CG must sit in front of Rocket Builder's CP by at least one body width (Lesson 8).
  • What to expect: A tumbles, C is steady, D wobbles or tumbles.
  • Extension: a Rocket Builder rocket with CG 253 mm and CP 289 mm from the tip, body 21.4 mm: (289 − 253) ÷ 21.4 ≈ 1.7 cal. Look up the Barrowman method, the hand calculation the app uses.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free lesson Fins and Finish Lines (apogeerockets.com/downloads/PDFs/58-3.pdf).
  • Nose weight setting: Advanced mode has Nose weight (clay), in grams. Add 5 g at a time and rebuild: the CG moves forward and the score rises, but the predicted altitude drops. A good trade-off discussion.
Lesson 4 of 9 · Grades 6–8

Drag and shape

Lesson 4

Time: about 55 minutes. Worksheet: W4 Drag test. Rocket Builder: Beginner mode; Advanced for the extension.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-ETS1-2 Evaluate competing designs with a systematic process.
MS-ETS1-3 Analyze test data to find the best features of several designs.
6.SP.B.4 (math) Display data (bar graph or dot plot).

Practice: analyzing and interpreting data. Crosscutting concept: structure and function.

I can…

  • I can list what makes drag bigger: speed, frontal area, shape and roughness.
  • I can run a fair test of nose shapes, changing one variable.
  • I can explain the fin trade-off: stability versus drag and weight.

Words to know

  • Drag: air resisting motion. Double the speed and drag goes up about 4 times.
  • Frontal area: the size of the front, seen from the nose.
  • Drag coefficient (Cd): a number for how slippery a shape is; model rockets are about 0.5–0.75.
  • Trade-off: improving one thing makes another worse.

Materials

Straw-rocket supplies, a squeeze-bottle launcher (bottle + straw sealed into the cap with clay), a tape measure, a kitchen scale (optional), safety glasses, W4.

Steps

1 Teach (10 minutes)

Read aloud

Four things make drag bigger: speed, a bigger front, a blunt shape, and roughness. Fins keep a rocket stable, but every fin adds drag and weight, so designers want fins that are just big enough. Today: change one thing at a time.

2 Nose-cone test (20 minutes)

  1. Three identical straw rockets: flat, cone and round noses, same mass (add tape to the lighter ones).
  2. Same launcher, same angle. 3 trials each. Mean and bar graph on W4.

3 Fin test (15 minutes)

No fins, 3 small, 3 medium, 4 large. 3 trials each. Record distance and "straight or wobbly".

4 Rocket Builder (5 minutes)

Keep everything the same and change only the nose shape; build each. The score barely changes: at model-rocket speeds the fins matter far more than the nose.

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

Which design had the best features? Could you combine them?

Check for understanding

  1. Name three things that make drag bigger.
  2. A rocket goes twice as fast. How much bigger is the drag?
  3. Why must the three rockets weigh the same?
  4. Why not put huge fins on every rocket?
Answer key
  1. Speed, frontal area, blunt shape, roughness (any three).
  2. About 4 times.
  3. So only the nose shape changes (a fair test).
  4. Extra drag and weight lower the flight, and it can be over-stable and turn into the wind.

Teacher notes

  • What to expect: flat noses go shortest; cone and round are often close, so small differences may be luck. More trials or a fairer launcher (a book dropped on the bottle from a set height) help.
  • Extension (Advanced mode): drag = ½ × 1.2 × speed² × Cd × area. A 30 mm rocket (area ≈ 0.00071 m²), Cd 0.65 at 50 m/s: ≈ 0.7 N, about its own weight. In Advanced → Flight, set the drag coefficient to 0.5 and then 0.8, build each time and compare the predicted altitude.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free lesson Rocket Optimization (apogeerockets.com/downloads/PDFs/58-2.pdf).
  • Change one thing: the launch simulator's change-one-thing experiments fly the same rocket with one setting changed (drag coefficient, nose, fins) and compare the flights.

Motors, impulse and energy

Lesson 5

Time: about 60 minutes. Worksheet: W5 Motor codes and impulse (W5b thrust curve for extension). Rocket Builder: Beginner mode, How high will it go?

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-PS2-2 A change in motion depends on the total force and the mass (thrust-to-weight, F = m × a).
MS-PS3-5 When an object's kinetic energy changes, energy was transferred to or from it.
MS-PS3-2 (connection) Higher up means more gravitational potential energy stored in the Earth–rocket system.
6.RP.A.3, 6.EE.A.2 (math) Ratios; evaluate impulse = average thrust × time.

Practice: using mathematics and computational thinking. Crosscutting concept: energy and matter.

I can…

  • I can name the parts of a model rocket motor and what each does.
  • I can decode a motor code like C6-5 and calculate total impulse.
  • I can trace energy through a flight: stored in the propellant, moving (kinetic), height (potential), and lost to drag as heat.

Words to know

  • Propellant, nozzle, delay, ejection charge: the parts of a motor, bottom to top.
  • Igniter: the wire that lights the motor. Adults only.
  • Total impulse (N·s): average thrust × burn time; the class letter (A, B, C…) gives its range. Each letter is up to twice the one before.
  • Kinetic energy: energy of motion. Gravitational potential energy: energy stored by height.

Materials

Calculators, W5. Optional: a sealed motor pack (adult shows it, unopened). Optional adult-led demo: film canisters with inside-snap lids, effervescent tablets, water, safety glasses.

Steps

1 Teach: inside a motor (10 minutes)

Read aloud

A model rocket motor is a factory-made paper tube: a nozzle, propellant that burns for about a second, a delay that makes a smoke trail while the rocket coasts, and an ejection charge that pops the nose and pushes out the parachute near apogee. Only adults handle motors and igniters.

In C6-5: C is the impulse class (5.01–10 N·s), 6 is the average thrust in newtons, 5 is the delay in seconds.

2 Decode and calculate (20 minutes)

Work through W5: decode four codes, find impulse = average thrust × burn time and the class, and check thrust-to-weight (100 g weighs about 1 N; it must be 5 or more).

3 Energy through a flight (15 minutes)

Draw a flight path. Label where energy is:

  • On the pad: stored in the propellant (chemical).
  • Boost: the motor transfers energy to the rocket: its speed and height grow, so its kinetic and potential energy grow.
  • Coast: kinetic energy turns into potential energy as it slows and climbs; drag turns some into heat in the air.
  • Apogee: the most potential energy, almost no kinetic.
  • Descent: the parachute's drag takes energy away, so it lands slowly.

4 Rocket Builder (10 minutes)

Build your rocket. Find How high will it go?: the recommended motor and predicted altitude. Use the speed and altitude there for the energy story.

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

Why do bigger motors need bigger fields?

Check for understanding

  1. In B6-4, what do the B, 6 and 4 mean?
  2. 5 N average for 2 s: total impulse and class?
  3. Rocket + motor weighs 1.2 N; average thrust 5 N. Pass the 5× rule?
  4. During the coast the rocket slows down. Where does its kinetic energy go?
  5. Who puts the igniter in the motor?
Answer key
  1. Class B (2.51–5 N·s), about 6 N average thrust, 4 s delay.
  2. 10 N·s, class C.
  3. No: 5 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 4.2.
  4. Mostly into gravitational potential energy (height); some into heat through drag.
  5. An adult. Always.

Teacher notes

  • Motor classes and NAR minimum fields: 1/2A and smaller 15 m (50 ft); A 30 m; B 60 m; C 120 m; D 150 m; E–F 300 m. Typical printed rockets: M13 15–150 m high, M18 60–300 m, M24 150–350 m, M29 300–750 m.
  • Optional adult-led fizzy demo (third law and energy from a reaction): an adult drops half a tablet into a film canister with a teaspoon of water, snaps the lid on, sets it lid-down and steps back; outside, glasses on, nobody leans over it; if it doesn't pop, wait 30 s. The adult keeps the tablets (Alka-Seltzer contains aspirin).
  • Extension (W5b): plot the B-motor thrust curve and find the area under it (≈ 4.3 N·s). F = m × a at liftoff: 0.086 kg rocket, 4.7 N average thrust: weight ≈ 0.84 N, net ≈ 3.9 N, a ≈ 45 m/s². Kinetic energy at 50 m/s: ½ × 0.086 × 50² ≈ 108 J; potential energy at 120 m: 0.086 × 9.8 × 120 ≈ 101 J. Where did the other energy go? (Drag.)
  • Students under 12 do the paper and math parts; the motor itself stays with an adult.
  • Energy from real numbers: the launch simulator's flight graphs show speed and altitude against time, and Download flight data (CSV) gives the numbers. Students compute kinetic energy (½mv²) and potential energy (mgh) at a few moments in a spreadsheet and graph both.

Design with a computer model

Lesson 6

Time: about 60 minutes (90 with Part C). Worksheet: W6 Design log. Rocket Builder: Beginner, then Advanced.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-ETS1-1 Define the criteria and constraints precisely, including effects on people and the environment.
MS-ETS1-2 Evaluate competing designs with a systematic process (a decision table).
MS-ETS1-4 Use a model to generate data, test and improve a design.
8.SP.A.1 (math) Scatter plot of two variables; describe the pattern.

Practice: developing and using models. Crosscutting concept: systems and system models.

I can…

  • I can write the criteria and constraints for a rocket.
  • I can use Rocket Builder to change one variable at a time and record the stability score.
  • I can pick a final design with a decision table and explain my choice with data.

Words to know

  • Criteria: what the design must do. Constraints: the limits.
  • Iteration: one trip around the design cycle (ask, imagine, plan, create, test, improve).
  • Model: something that stands in for the real thing so you can test it. Rocket Builder is a computer model.

Materials

Computers or tablets with Rocket Builder (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net), W6. 12+ track: a 3D printer and filament (adult runs it), or an order.

Steps

1 Define the problem (10 minutes)

Criteria: score 1–2 cal (2–3 OK) · at least one motor gets ✓ Good (5× thrust-to-weight, 15 m/s off the rod, 30 m high) · the predicted flight fits our field. Constraints: our printer's height · our field size · motor cost (the smallest motor that works) · safety: no people, roads, dry grass or trees near the pad.

2 Part A: one variable in Beginner mode (20 minutes)

  1. Pick a motor size (M18 is common) and your printer height. Keep fins and nose fixed.
  2. Start at the shortest height; build; record height, score, best motor, altitude, power and "fins grew?" (Grown-up details → Build notes).
  3. Add about 50 mm each time, 5 heights. Plot height vs score on W6.

3 Part B: Advanced mode (15 minutes)

Switch to Advanced. Under Fins, tick Custom fin size and change only the span (15, 20, 25, 30 mm). Find where the score crosses 1.0.

4 Choose (10 minutes)

Score your top 3 designs in the decision table (stability, altitude fits field, print time, looks). Pick one; write a sentence using your data. Save it, then ▶ Watch it launch.

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

What can't the model tell you? (Real print mass, glue, paint, wind, motor-to-motor variation.)

Check for understanding

  1. What's the difference between a criterion and a constraint?
  2. Why change one thing at a time?
  3. Your design scores 0.8 cal. Name two fixes.
  4. Your design scores 3.6 cal. Problem?
Answer key
  1. A criterion is what it must do; a constraint is a limit.
  2. To know which change caused the result.
  3. A taller body; bigger or swept fins; nose weight; a lighter motor.
  4. A small one: over-stable, it weathercocks into the wind. Smaller fins or a shorter body.

Teacher notes

  • What to expect: with the same fins, taller rockets score higher. Very short ones show "fins grew" (the app enlarged them) and a score just above 1. In one test (M18, trapezoid, body 250 mm): span 15 mm gave −0.5 cal, 25 mm 1.3, 30 mm 1.7. A negative score means the CP is in front of the CG.
  • 12+ track: when the design is chosen, download the ZIP and an adult starts printing (parts print for several hours). Or order it printed (an adult needs an account). Assemble with an adult by Lesson 8, following the build notes and the assembly guide.
  • Students under 12 keep their design on screen (no download, print or order).
  • Environment connection (optional): the constraint "impacts on people and the natural environment" covers the launch site (fire risk, litter, wildlife) and material use: a printed rocket uses about 50–100 g of PLA; failed prints are waste. Students can add a constraint about it.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free lessons for grades 5–8 (apogeerockets.com/Teach-with-Model-Rockets) use kit rockets; Rocket Optimization (58-2) fits here.
  • Class codes: sign in and make a class code at rocket.quiverdesignworks.net/classes. Students hand in their final design with the code and a nickname (no student accounts), so you can compare the class's designs in one list. Each design also has a printable mission card (designer ▾ download menu → Mission card).
Lesson 7 of 9 · Grades 6–8

Parachutes and landing energy

Lesson 7

Time: about 60 minutes. Worksheet: W7 Parachute drop test. Rocket Builder: Which parachute? card.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-PS3-1 Graph data to show how kinetic energy depends on mass and on speed.
MS-PS2-2 Balanced forces (drag = weight) mean a steady speed.
MS-ETS1-3 Analyze test data to find the best design features.
7.RP.A.2 (math) Proportional relationships: drift = time × wind speed.

Practice: analyzing and interpreting data. Crosscutting concept: scale, proportion and quantity.

I can…

  • I can explain terminal speed: a falling object stops speeding up when drag equals weight.
  • I can calculate descent rate (height ÷ time) from a drop test.
  • I can use a graph to show that kinetic energy grows faster than speed, and explain why a rocket must land slowly.

Words to know

  • Descent rate: falling speed, m/s. Terminal speed: the steady speed when drag balances weight.
  • Kinetic energy: KE = ½ × mass × speed². Double the speed, four times the energy.
  • Drift: how far the wind carries the rocket sideways while it comes down.

Materials

Plastic bags, string, tape, identical washers, stopwatches, a tape measure, a 2–3 m drop spot (an adult drops), W7. Optional: a paper or plastic streamer.

Steps

1 Teach (10 minutes)

Read aloud

As something falls, drag grows with speed until it equals the weight. Then the forces balance and the speed stops changing: terminal speed. A parachute makes a lot of drag, so the balance comes at a slow speed. Rocket Builder picks the smallest standard chute (12, 15, 18, 24… inches) that brings your rocket down at about 4.5 m/s or less. Too big a chute hangs in the air longer and drifts farther.

2 Drop test (25 minutes)

  1. Make 20, 30 and 40 cm parachutes with 30 cm lines and the same washers.
  2. An adult drops each from the same height; time 3 trials each; also the washers alone (and a streamer, if you made one).
  3. Mean time and descent rate (height ÷ mean time) for each. Graph canopy size vs time.

3 Landing energy (15 minutes)

On W7, fill the kinetic energy table for an 80 g (0.08 kg) rocket landing at 2, 4, 8 and 16 m/s, and plot KE against speed. Then for 40, 80 and 160 g at 4 m/s, plot KE against mass. Which graph is a straight line?

4 Rocket Builder (5 minutes)

Find Which parachute?: size and descent speed. Time down = altitude ÷ descent speed; drift in a 4.5 m/s breeze = time × 4.5.

5 Wrap up (5 minutes)

Why does landing at 16 m/s break a rocket when 4 m/s doesn't?

Check for understanding

  1. A parachute falls 3 m in 1.5 s. Descent rate?
  2. At terminal speed, which two forces are balanced?
  3. Landing speed doubles from 4 to 8 m/s. What happens to the kinetic energy?
  4. From 100 m at 4 m/s in a 4.5 m/s wind: how far does it drift?
Answer key
  1. 2 m/s.
  2. Drag (up) and weight (down).
  3. It becomes 4 times bigger.
  4. 100 ÷ 4 = 25 s; 25 × 4.5 ≈ 113 m.

Teacher notes

  • KE table answers (0.08 kg): 2 m/s 0.16 J; 4 m/s 0.64 J; 8 m/s 2.56 J; 16 m/s 10.24 J (a curve). Mass at 4 m/s: 40 g 0.32 J; 80 g 0.64 J; 160 g 1.28 J (a straight line). A landing at 4.5 m/s is like jumping off a step about 1 m high; 16 m/s is like falling about 13 m, off a four-storey roof.
  • Extension: descent speed = √(2mg ÷ (1.2 × Cd × area)), Cd ≈ 0.75. A 76 g rocket under a 15-inch chute: ≈ 3.8 m/s, the app's number. Doubling the chute's width halves the speed.
  • 12+ track: get the parachute size the app says (or make one), and practise folding it: in half twice, lines wrapped loosely, rolled to slide easily into the body tube. Flame-resistant wadding goes below it on launch day.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free lesson The Optimum Descent Challenge (apogeerockets.com/downloads/PDFs/58-4.pdf).
  • Parachute or streamer: the launch simulator flies the rocket with either, showing descent speed and drift. Its descent graph is a clean terminal-speed curve for the KE discussion.
Lesson 8 of 9 · Grades 6–8

Launch prep: safety, tracking and preflight

Lesson 8

Time: about 90 minutes, or two class periods. Worksheets: W8 Altitude tracking, the inclinometer template, W8b Safety rules, W8c Launch checklists. Rocket Builder: How big a launch field? card.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-ETS1-1 Define constraints precisely, including safety and effects on people and the environment.
MS-PS2-2 Plan an investigation (here, how to measure the flight).
7.G.A.1, 7.RP.A.2 (math) Scale drawings; proportional reasoning (height = baseline × multiplier).

Practice: planning and carrying out investigations. Crosscutting concept: scale, proportion and quantity.

I can…

  • I can explain the main rules of the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code and why each matters.
  • I can build an inclinometer and use it to find the height of something tall.
  • I can run a preflight check, including the balance-point go/no-go test.

Words to know

  • Safety code: the rules everyone at a launch follows.
  • Safety key: no key in the controller, no launch. Misfire: the button is pressed but the motor doesn't light.
  • LCO / RSO: the adults in charge of the launch button and of checking rockets.
  • Angle of elevation, baseline: how steeply you look up; your measured distance from the pad.

Materials

Inclinometer template (printed at 100%), cardstock, a straw, string, a washer, tape, a long tape measure, calculators, W8, W8b, W8c. 12+ track: the assembled rocket, a kitchen scale, string, a zip bag and some US nickels, one motor (adult handles it, no igniter), the launch controller with no igniter attached.

Steps

1 The safety code (20 minutes)

Read the safety page together. For each rule, ask why? Do the "Is it safe?" stories on W8b. Assign jobs: adults are LCO and RSO and handle motors, igniters and the key; students call the countdown, track, record and recover.

Misfire drill: with no motor and no igniter, set up the pad and controller. "Range is clear! 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" The adult pretends it didn't go, takes out the key, and everyone counts 60 seconds before anyone walks up.

2 Inclinometer and a tall object (30 minutes)

  1. Build the inclinometer from the template (straw on the top edge, weighted string at the pivot). Level = 0°.
  2. Measure a 20 m baseline from a tree or pole. Read the angle to the top 3 times; average.
  3. Height = baseline × multiplier (table on W8) + eye height. Check with a second baseline.

3 Preflight checks (12+ track, 30 minutes)

  1. Weigh the rocket without a motor; compare with the app.
  2. Look it over: fins firm, shock cord tied, parachute folded and not torn.
  3. Nose fit (coin breakout test; do it during assembly, before the body sections are glued): tie a cord to the nose's tie bar, push the nose into its body section, hold the section nose-down and hang a zip bag on the cord. Add nickels (5 g each) until the nose pulls out. About 4–5 nickels is right for an M18 Starter rocket; Rocket Builder's build notes give the target. Too few: tape the shoulder; too many: sand it.
  4. Balance point, go/no-go: an adult puts a motor in (no igniter). Balance the rocket on a finger and measure from the nose tip. Go only if this CG is at least one body width in front of the app's CP. The adult takes the motor out again.
  5. Field check: compare the app's How big a launch field? with your field. Too small? Pick a smaller motor that still gets a ✓, or go to a club launch.

4 Wrap up (10 minutes)

Check the wind forecast: under 20 mph (under 10 is better). Pack the launch box (W8c).

Check for understanding

  1. How far back must everyone stand for a D motor or smaller?
  2. After a misfire, what two things happen before anyone goes to the pad?
  3. Baseline 50 m, angle 60° (multiplier 1.73), eye height 1.5 m. How high?
  4. The measured CG is 10 mm behind the app's CP. Go or no-go?
Answer key
  1. At least 15 feet (about 5 m).
  2. The key comes out (or the battery is disconnected), then everyone waits 60 seconds.
  3. 50 × 1.73 + 1.5 ≈ 88 m.
  4. No-go: the CG must be in front of the CP by at least one body width. Add nose weight or bigger fins, and check again.

Teacher notes

  • Tracking on launch day: use a baseline of about half to all of the predicted altitude (angles between 30° and 60° are most accurate), stand to the side of the wind, and use two trackers on different sides.
  • Extension (tangent): height = baseline × tan(angle) + eye height. A 2° error at 45° changes a 50 m-baseline height by about 7%; at 75° by about 16%, so long baselines are better.
  • Swing test: students can swing-test the real rocket (motor in, no igniter) as a demonstration, but the balance-point check is the go/no-go.
  • Assembly happens out of class with an adult, following Rocket Builder's build notes and the assembly guide. Adults use the glue.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free newsletter Tracking Model Rockets (apogeerockets.com/education/downloads/Newsletter222.pdf) and their Teaching Tips page (apogeerockets.com/Edu/Teaching_Tips) for running a launch with a class (two pads, separate launch and recovery crews, the adult installs igniters).
  • Altitude calculator: rocket.quiverdesignworks.net/tools/altitude does height from angle and distance, shows the working, the 45° rule and the average of two stations. Use it to check hand calculations, not replace them. The simulator's launch angle setting (0–30°) shows how a tilted rod changes where the rocket lands.

Launch day and mission report

Lesson 9

Time: a morning or afternoon outside, plus one period for the mission report. Worksheets: F Flight log, W9 Graph your data, C Mission report planner and rubric. Rocket Builder: predicted altitude.

Standards

Code In plain words
MS-ETS1-4 Use a model's predictions, compare with a real test, and improve the design.
MS-ETS1-3 Analyze flight data to find what to keep and what to change.
MS-PS3-2 (connection) At apogee the Earth–rocket system stores the most gravitational potential energy.
7.RP.A.3, 6.SP.B.5 (math) Percent difference; mean of tracker readings.
WHST.6-8.2, SL.6.4–SL.8.4 (ELA) Write and present an explanation with data.

Practice: analyzing and interpreting data; obtaining, evaluating and communicating information. Crosscutting concept: systems and system models.

I can…

  • I can follow the launch-day rules and do my team job.
  • I can measure the altitude and compare it with Rocket Builder's prediction (percent difference).
  • I can explain the differences and propose one testable change.

Words to know

  • Prediction: written down before the flight.
  • Percent difference: (predicted − measured) ÷ predicted × 100.
  • Flight log: the record of each flight.

Materials

Launch box: the rocket with the parachute packed and spare wadding; motors and igniters (an adult carries them); pad, rod, blast deflector; controller, safety key (adult's pocket), fresh batteries; inclinometers, tape measure, stopwatches; safety glasses; a bucket of water or extinguisher; F flight log on a clipboard. Warm-up: stomp rockets.

Steps

1 Predict (before you go)

Write Rocket Builder's predicted altitude, the motor, and the time to apogee on the flight log. Each student writes their own guess too. (The rocket's mission card and the simulator's Predict, fly, compare log have these numbers.)

2 Launch (adult-run, students on jobs)

  1. Check wind (under 20 mph), the field and the pad area (no dry grass).
  2. RSO check: rocket sound, preflight done in Lesson 8.
  3. Wadding, parachute and cord in; nose on. (Students can help.)
  4. Adult: igniter into the motor, motor into the rocket, retainer locked; rocket on the rod; with the key out, clip the wires.
  5. Everyone back at least 15 ft; trackers at their baselines.
  6. Adult: key in. Countdown caller: "Range is clear! 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, launch!" Adult presses, then takes the key out.
  7. Trackers pinch their strings at apogee; timers time liftoff→parachute and parachute→landing.
  8. Recovery crew goes only when the adult says so.

Misfire: key out, wait 60 seconds.

3 Compare (on the field or back in class)

  1. Altitude = baseline × multiplier + eye height; average the trackers. Check with the altitude calculator (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net/tools/altitude).
  2. Percent difference from the prediction.
  3. Why different? Mass, paint and glue drag, wind tilt, tracking error, motor variation.

4 Mission report (one period)

Poster, slides, written report or a 3–5 minute talk with the parts on planner C: mission, design (with CG, CP and score), the science (third law, stability, parachute), prediction vs flight (table or graph from W9), what happened and why, one change for next time, two safety rules.

Check for understanding

  1. Put in order: apogee, liftoff, burnout, landing, parachute opens.
  2. Two trackers measured 119 m and 138 m. Mean?
  3. Predicted 150 m, measured 120 m. Percent difference?
  4. Where is the rocket's gravitational potential energy greatest?
  5. Who removes the spent motor?
Answer key
  1. Liftoff, burnout, apogee, parachute opens, landing.
  2. (119 + 138) ÷ 2 = 128.5 m.
  3. 30 ÷ 150 × 100 = 20%.
  4. At apogee.
  5. An adult, after it cools.

Teacher notes

  • Predict, fly, compare: the prediction must be written before the flight. If the rocket can fly again, change one thing (motor, nose weight, parachute) and predict again.
  • First flight on the smallest motor that gets a ✓ in Rocket Builder. A rocket that comes back in good shape can fly again with a new motor and fresh wadding.
  • Under-12 students are trackers, recorders, timers and countdown callers; the motor flight itself is 12+, and adults handle all motors and igniters. Stomp rockets are a good warm-up for everyone.
  • No field or bad weather? Fly at a local NAR club launch (nar.org), or use Rocket Builder's simulated launch and do the report with the predicted numbers and the stomp-rocket data.
  • Rubric: five rows (science, design process, data and math, explaining results, communication and safety), 1–4 each, 20 points. It's on planner C.
  • Further resources: Apogee Components' free Flight Record data sheet (apogeerockets.com/Rocket_Books_Videos/Data_Sheets/Flight_Record_Data_Sheet) is another flight log format.
  • Launch-day kit from Rocket Builder: print each rocket's mission card (designer ▾ download menu → Mission card) for the clipboard; use the simulator's Predict, fly, compare log to record the prediction before the flight and the measured altitude after (Download log (CSV) for the report); and check tracker maths with the altitude calculator (rocket.quiverdesignworks.net/tools/altitude).
